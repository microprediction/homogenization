Options on bonds under regime switching

A call on a zero-coupon bond when the short rate's mean level and volatility follow a hidden regime.

The model

The short rate $x_t$ is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process whose mean level and volatility are set by a hidden regime $y_t \in \{1, 2\}$ that switches at rate $\lambda$ in each direction:

$$dx_t = \kappa\,(\theta_{y_t} - x_t)\,dt + \sigma_{y_t}\,dW_t .$$

The rate reverts over $1/\kappa$ and the regime changes every $1/\lambda$.

Parameters: $\kappa = 0.5$, $\theta = (0.05, 0.03)$, $\sigma = (0.015, 0.010)$, $x_0 = 0.04$, and the economy starts in regime 1.

The quantity

The price of a call, expiring at $T = 1$ with strike $K$, on the zero-coupon bond maturing at $S = 4$:

$$C = \mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\int_0^T x_r\,dr}\,\big(P(T, S) - K\big)^+\Big].$$

The bond price at expiry depends on the regime then: $P(T,S) = A_{y_T}\,e^{-b\,x_T}$ with $b = (1 - e^{-\kappa(S-T)})/\kappa$ and $A_j$ the regime-$j$ factor for the remaining three years.

Averaging

When the regime switches infinitely fast the limit is Vasicek's model with averaged mean level and variance, and the option has Jamshidian's closed-form price.

Reduction to a linear system

In regime $j$ the call pays when $x_T < x_j^* = \log(A_j/K)/b$, so the price combines the quantities

$$\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\int_0^T x_r\,dr - c\,x_T}\,\mathbf 1\{x_T < x_j^*,\ y_T = j\}\big]$$

for $c = b$ and $c = 0$. Each follows by Gil-Pelaez inversion from the same expectation without the indicator on $x_T$ and with $c$ replaced by $c - iu$.

For such an expectation the Feynman–Kac equation is the same as for the bond price, and the same substitution $e^{-\tilde B(t)x}a_i(t)$ works. The terms in $x$ again require $\tilde B' = 1 - \kappa\tilde B$, but now the start is $\tilde B(0) = c$, because the payoff contains $e^{-c\,x}$. So the coefficient of $x$ is

$$\tilde B(t) = c\,e^{-\kappa t} + (1 - e^{-\kappa t})/\kappa,$$

which does not depend on the regime, so

$$\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\int_0^t x_r\,dr - c\,x_t}\,\mathbf 1\{y_t = j\} \mid y_0 = i\big] = e^{-\tilde B(t)x_0}\,a_i(t), \qquad a' = (Q + \operatorname{diag} g)\,a, \quad a(0) = e_j,$$

with

$$g_i = -\kappa\theta_i\tilde B + \tfrac12\sigma_i^2\tilde B^2.$$

The terminal vector is now the indicator $e_j$ of the regime at expiry rather than $\mathbf 1$.

The expansion

With $\varepsilon = 1/\lambda$, split the terminal vector into its stationary part and a remainder:

$$e_j = \tfrac12\,\mathbf 1 + \tfrac12\,s_j\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad s_1 = +1,\quad s_2 = -1 .$$

The stationary part is carried by the outer series exactly as for a bond. The remainder relaxes like $e^{-2\lambda t}$, but on the way it leaves a first-order imprint through $\tilde g(0)$, which is not zero here because $\tilde B(0) = c$. To first order, with the upper sign for a start in regime 1,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\int_0^T x_r\,dr - c\,x_T}\,\mathbf 1\{y_T = j\}\big] \;=\; &\tfrac12\,e^{-\tilde B(T)\,x_0}\,\exp\Big(\int_0^T \bar g + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_0^T \tilde g^{\,2}\Big) \\ &\times\Big(1 \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde g(T) + s_j\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde g(0)\Big) + O(\varepsilon^2) + O(e^{-2\lambda T}). \end{aligned}$$

The term in $\tilde g(T)$ is the memory of the regime at the start. The term in $\tilde g(0)$ is its mirror image: the memory of the regime at expiry, which decides the bond price the option is written on.

The expansion needs $|\tilde g|/\lambda$ to be small at the frequencies $u$ that matter. The Gil-Pelaez integrals therefore stop at eight standard deviations of $x_T$ in frequency.

The option price in closed form

Every integral above is elementary, and to first order the option price reduces to Gaussian integrals.

Step 1: the averaged model

Under the averaged model's $T$-forward measure the rate at expiry is Gaussian, $x_T \sim N(\mu, v)$, with

$$\begin{aligned} \mu &= E\,x_0 + \kappa\bar\theta\,B - \bar s\,M_{1,1}, \qquad v = \bar s\,M_{2,0}, \\ \bar P(0, T) &= \exp\big(-B\,x_0 - \kappa\bar\theta\,M_{0,1} + \tfrac12\bar s\,M_{0,2}\big), \\ E &= e^{-\kappa T}, \qquad B = \frac{1 - E}{\kappa}, \end{aligned}$$

where the building blocks are

$$M_{k,m} = \int_0^T e^{-k\kappa t}\,B(t)^m\,dt = \frac{1}{\kappa^m}\sum_{l=0}^{m}\binom{m}{l}(-1)^l\,\Phi_{k+l}, \qquad \Phi_0 = T,\quad \Phi_n = \frac{1 - e^{-n\kappa T}}{n\kappa}.$$

Step 2: the correction polynomial

Because $\tilde B(t) = c\,e^{-\kappa t} + B(t)$, every correction term is a polynomial in $c$. The first-order factor is

$$\begin{aligned} \Pi_j(c) \;=\; &\tfrac12\Big(\kappa^2\tilde\theta^2\,J_2(c) - \kappa\tilde\theta\,\tilde s\,J_3(c) + \tfrac14\tilde s^2 J_4(c)\Big) \\ &\pm \tfrac12\Big(-\kappa\tilde\theta\,(B + Ec) + \tfrac12\tilde s\,(B + Ec)^2\Big) + \tfrac12\,s_j\Big(-\kappa\tilde\theta\,c + \tfrac12\tilde s\,c^2\Big), \end{aligned}$$ $$J_n(c) = \int_0^T \tilde B(t)^n\,dt = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\,M_{k,\,n-k}\;c^k .$$

Write $\Pi_j(c) = \sum_{k=0}^{4}\pi_{jk}\,c^k$. A factor $c^k$ on the transform is the $k$-th derivative of the Gaussian density of $x_T$.

Step 3: the bond at expiry

In regime $j$ the bond price at expiry is $A_j e^{-b x_T}$, with $b = (1 - e^{-\kappa\tau})/\kappa$ and $\tau = S - T$. The regime-switching bond formula gives

$$A_{1,2} = \exp\Big(-\kappa\bar\theta\,I_1 + \tfrac12\bar s\,I_2 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(\kappa^2\tilde\theta^2 I_2 - \kappa\tilde\theta\,\tilde s\,I_3 + \tfrac14\tilde s^2 I_4\big)\Big) \Big(1 \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(-\kappa\tilde\theta\,b + \tfrac12\tilde s\,b^2\big)\Big),$$

with $I_k = M_{0,k}$ evaluated at maturity $\tau$ in place of $T$.

Step 4: the price

$$C \;=\; \bar P(0, T)\sum_{j = 1}^{2}\tfrac12\Big(R_0(A_j) + \varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{4}(-1)^k\,\pi_{jk}\,R_k(A_j)\Big) + O(\varepsilon^2),$$ $$\begin{aligned} R_0(A) &= A\,e^{-b\mu + \frac12 b^2 v}\,\Phi(z^* + b\sqrt v) - K\,\Phi(z^*), \\ R_k(A) &= v^{-k/2}\Big(A\,e^{-b\mu + \frac12 b^2 v}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\big(-b\sqrt v\big)^{k-i} H_i\big(z^* + b\sqrt v\big) - K\,H_k(z^*)\Big), \\ z^* &= \frac{\log(A/K)/b - \mu}{\sqrt v}, \qquad H_0 = \Phi, \qquad H_i(w) = -\mathrm{He}_{i-1}(w)\,\varphi(w) \ \ (i \ge 1). \end{aligned}$$

Here $\mathrm{He}_n$ are the Hermite polynomials $1, w, w^2 - 1, \dots$, and $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ are the standard normal distribution and density. $R_0$ is Jamshidian's formula, so with $\varepsilon = 0$ this is the averaged model's option price. The $\varepsilon$ terms are the new correction.

Thesis erratum: deterministic shifts and forward measures

Appendix B, Section B.4 of the linked 2001 thesis has three source-formula errors: its shifted caplet and floorlet omit discounting from today to the fixing date, its shifted swaplet applies one maturity's deterministic factor to both legs, and its futures recursion has a positive discount exponent. These are errata for the thesis; the bond-option implementation above does not use those formulas.

A deterministic-shift identity

Let $r^\phi_u=r_u+\phi(u)$, where $\phi$ is deterministic and integrable, and put

$$A(t,u)=\exp\left\{-\int_t^u\phi(v)\,dv\right\}.$$

Pathwise, the shifted discount factor is $D^\phi(t,u)=A(t,u)D^0(t,u)$. Consequently, under the same risk-neutral measure, every shifted zero bond satisfies the exact identity

$$P^\phi(t,u)=A(t,u)P^0(t,u).$$

No Gaussian, affine or independence assumption is needed for this step.

A payoff-transport theorem

The same identity handles a nonlinear claim on several bond maturities at once. Let $u_1,\ldots,u_d\ge T$ and let $\Psi$ be any Borel payoff for which the displayed expectations exist. Then

$$\begin{aligned} V_t^\phi[\Psi] &=\mathbb E_t^Q\!\left[D^\phi(t,T) \Psi\!\left(P^\phi(T,u_1),\ldots,P^\phi(T,u_d)\right)\right]\\ &=A(t,T)\,\mathbb E_t^Q\!\left[D^0(t,T) \Psi\!\left(A(T,u_1)P^0(T,u_1),\ldots, A(T,u_d)P^0(T,u_d)\right)\right]. \end{aligned}$$

At the path level, if $\Gamma$ is any integrable functional of the short-rate trajectory up to $T$, the corresponding identity is simply

$$V_t^\phi[\Gamma] =A(t,T)\,\mathbb E_t^Q\!\left[D^0(t,T) \Gamma\!\left((r_s+\phi(s))_{t\le s\le T}\right)\right].$$

Thus deterministic shifting preserves a path-dependent pricing problem after a deterministic translation of every rate argument; it does not generally preserve the payoff unchanged.

This is an exact transport rule: discounting to exercise contributes $A(t,T)$, while every underlying bond argument receives the factor for its own maturity. If $\Psi$ is positively homogeneous of degree one and all arguments receive the same factor $a$, the result reduces to the common prefactor $A(t,T)a$. With different maturities the factors are generally different, so they must instead be absorbed into the payoff weights or strikes.

There is also a sharp converse. Suppose one scalar $c$ multiplied the unshifted value of every linear portfolio of bonds maturing at $u_1,\ldots,u_d$. Applying that assertion to each single-bond portfolio forces $c=A(t,u_j)$ for every $j$. Hence a universal common factor exists if and only if all relevant maturity factors coincide. The swaplet error below is the two-maturity counterexample.

This payoff transport is the elementary pricing mechanism behind the deterministic-shift extensions developed systematically by Brigo and Mercurio (2001).

Forward measures are invariant

The deterministic shift changes bond levels but not the forward measures themselves. For any numeraire maturity $U$ and $s\le U$, the density process relative to the common risk-neutral measure is

$$\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{dQ^{U,\phi}}{dQ}\right|_{\mathcal F_s} &=\frac{D^\phi(0,s)P^\phi(s,U)}{P^\phi(0,U)}\\ &=\frac{A(0,s)A(s,U)}{A(0,U)} \frac{D^0(0,s)P^0(s,U)}{P^0(0,U)}\\ &=\left.\frac{dQ^{U,0}}{dQ}\right|_{\mathcal F_s}. \end{aligned}$$

The cancellation uses only the cocycle identity $A(0,s)A(s,U)=A(0,U)$. Thus every conditional law under a bond-forward measure is exactly the same before and after a deterministic additive shift.

This gives a useful option corollary. For $T\le u,U$, put $X_T^0=P^0(T,u)/P^0(T,U)$ and $c=A(T,u)/A(T,U)$. Then $X_T^\phi=cX_T^0$. If the strike is transformed as $K^\phi=cK^0$,

$$\frac{V_t^\phi}{P^\phi(t,U)} =\mathbb E^{Q^U}_t[(cX_T^0-cK^0)^+] =c\frac{V_t^0}{P^0(t,U)}.$$

Because the Black formula is homogeneous in forward, strike and option value, the corresponding Black implied volatility is unchanged. This is an invariance at matched transformed moneyness; holding the numeric strike fixed changes moneyness and need not preserve implied volatility.

Caplets and floorlets

Take $t\le T\le\tau<\tau+\Delta$ and write $q=1+\Delta K$. For the generalized bond-option positive part used in Appendix B, define

$$q^\phi=q\,\frac{A(T,\tau+\Delta)}{A(T,\tau)}.$$

Substitution of the pathwise discount identity gives

$$\begin{aligned} C^\phi_t &=\mathbb E_t^Q\!\left[ D^\phi(t,T)\{P^\phi(T,\tau)-qP^\phi(T,\tau+\Delta)\}^+\right]\\ &=A(t,T)A(T,\tau)\, \mathbb E_t^Q\!\left[ D^0(t,T)\{P^0(T,\tau)-q^\phi P^0(T,\tau+\Delta)\}^+\right]\\ &=A(t,\tau)\,C^0_t(q^\phi). \end{aligned}$$

The floorlet has the same prefactor with the positive part reversed. The formulas on printed pp. 172–173 retain $A(T,\tau)$ but omit $A(t,T)$; whenever the corresponding generalized option has positive value, they therefore differ by the factor $A(t,T)^{-1}$ and are too high when the integrated shift from $t$ to $T$ is positive.

Swaplets

Linearity requires each zero bond to carry the factor for its own payment date:

$$S^\phi_t=A(t,\tau)P^0(t,\tau) -qA(t,\tau+\Delta)P^0(t,\tau+\Delta).$$

Printed p. 175 instead multiplies both terms by $A(t,\tau)$. This cannot be a harmless common normalization because the two cashflows occur at different dates.

The futures recursion

Let $Q^{T_i}$ denote the $T_i$-forward measure. Bayes' rule for the bond numeraire gives the exact one-step identity

$$\mathbb E^{Q^{T_i}}\!\left[f_i\mid\mathcal F_{T_{i-1}}\right] =\frac{1}{P(T_{i-1},T_i)} \mathbb E^Q\!\left[ e^{-\int_{T_{i-1}}^{T_i}r_u\,du}f_i \,\middle|\,\mathcal F_{T_{i-1}}\right].$$

The exponent is negative. Printed p. 177 uses a positive exponent while retaining $P(T_{i-1},T_i)^{-1}$. If its expectation were already under $Q^{T_i}$, both the discount factor and the outside bond normalization would instead be absent. This is precisely the change-of-numeraire theorem formalized by Geman, El Karoui and Rochet (1995).

Exact regression checks

For $t=0$, $T=\tau=1$, $\Delta=1$, $K=0$, zero base rate and $\phi=0.05$, the correct caplet and swaplet value is

$$e^{-0.05}-e^{-0.10}=0.0463920065.$$

The printed caplet formula gives $1-e^{-0.05}=0.0487705755$, while the printed swaplet gives zero. With $q=1.1$, an in-the-money floorlet is $0.0440917353$ rather than $0.0463523670$.
