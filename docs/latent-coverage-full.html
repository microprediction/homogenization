Conditional coverage behind exact randomized ranks

A latent iid representation preserves marginal ranks, but replaces the iid beta coverage law by a conditional-expectation contraction.

Specify the information being conditioned on

Given a latent variable $U$, let calibration scores $S_1,\ldots,S_N$ be iid with a random continuous CDF $G_U$. Assume the test score is independent of this panel given $U$, with conditional CDF

$$F_{{\rm test}\mid U}=(1-\delta)G_U+\delta B_U,\qquad0\le\delta<1,$$

where $B_U$ is a genuine CDF and $\delta$ is deterministic. Write $Q_k$ for the $k$th calibration order statistic, $p=k/(N+1)$, and

$$\mathcal D=\sigma(S_1,\ldots,S_N),\qquad C=\Pr(S_{\rm test}\le Q_k\mid\mathcal D).$$

The score map and rank are fixed before seeing the panel. For randomized-age calibration, the labels here are the independent unsorted age labels: $\mathcal D$ excludes ages and the chronological sorting permutation. The same conclusions hold when conditioning only on $Q_k$.

Beta contraction, not an exact beta law

The conditional probability integral transform gives $Z=G_U(Q_k)\sim\operatorname{Beta}(k,N+1-k)$, even after averaging over $U$. Conditional test independence and the tower property give

$$C=(1-\delta)M+\delta R,\qquad M=\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D],\qquad R=\mathbb E[B_U(Q_k)\mid\mathcal D]\in[0,1].$$

For every convex function $\psi$ for which the expectations exist, conditional Jensen gives $\mathbb E\psi(M)\le\mathbb E\psi(Z)$. This is convex-order domination, which does not order individual lower-tail probabilities.

In particular, if $\delta=0$, the marginal coverage is exactly $p$ and

$$\mathbb E C=p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(C)\le\frac{p(1-p)}{N+2}.$$

The variance cannot exceed the iid beta variance, yet $C$ need not itself be beta. No analogous variance assertion is made for $\delta>0$.

Repeated use: exact marginal law, dependent conditional errors

Now set $\delta=0$ and suppose that, given $U$ and the panel, $L$ future scores are iid from $G_U$. Put $I_\ell=\mathbf1_{\{S_{N+\ell}\le Q_k\}}$, $K_L=\sum_{\ell=1}^L I_\ell$, and $V_{\mathcal D}=\operatorname{Var}(Z\mid\mathcal D)$.

Conditionally on the realized score panel, posterior uncertainty about $U$ creates the exact decomposition

$$\mathbb E[K_L\mid\mathcal D]=LM,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(K_L\mid\mathcal D) =LM(1-M)+L(L-1)V_{\mathcal D},$$ $$\operatorname{Cov}(I_\ell,I_r\mid\mathcal D)=V_{\mathcal D}, \qquad \ell\ne r.$$

Marginally, $Z$ is beta and $K_L$ is beta-binomial, so

$$\operatorname{Var}(K_L)=Lp(1-p)\frac{N+1+L}{N+2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(M)+\mathbb E V_{\mathcal D} =\frac{p(1-p)}{N+2}.$$

The second identity splits the universal beta variability into between-panel coverage variability and within-panel posterior latent uncertainty. It does not make the realized-panel coverage beta or future errors conditionally independent.

In the $N=k=9$ stationary witness below, $\mathbb E V_{\mathcal D}=0.000921316035$. For $L=20$, the mean conditional variance is $2.004890050279$, the between-panel component is $2.904200858812$, and their sum is the beta-binomial variance $4.909090909091$.

A valid finite-sample lower-tail budget

For $Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$, with $a=k$, $b=N+1-k$ and $p=a/(a+b)$, define the stop-loss function

$$L_{a,b}(t)=\mathbb E(t-Z)_+ =t I_t(a,b)-p I_t(a+1,b),\qquad0\le t\le1,$$

where $I_t$ is the regularized incomplete beta function. For $0<x<(1-\delta)p$, set $y=x/(1-\delta)$; then

$$\boxed{\Pr(C\le x)\le \inf_{y<t\le1}\frac{L_{a,b}(t)}{t-y}.}$$

Indeed, $C\ge(1-\delta)M$ and $(t-y)\mathbf1_{\{M\le y\}}\le(t-M)_+$. Taking expectations and using conditional Jensen proves the bound.

For the maximum calibration score, $k=N$, the beta stop-loss is $t^{N+1}/(N+1)$. Minimization at $t=y/p$ gives the explicit budget

$$\Pr(C\le x)\le\left(\frac{x}{(1-\delta)p}\right)^N, \qquad0<x<(1-\delta)p,\quad p=\frac N{N+1}.$$

Both contraction envelopes are sharp

The stop-loss bound is exact over all conditional-mean contractions of the beta variable. More generally, let $Z$ have a continuous positive density on $(0,1)$ and mean $p$, put $M=\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D]$, and fix $0<y<p$.

The lower-truncated mean is continuous and strictly increasing from zero to $p$. Hence there is a unique $t_y\in(y,1)$ satisfying

$$\mathbb E[Z\mid Z\le t_y]=y.$$

For $L(t)=\mathbb E(t-Z)_+$, differentiation gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{L(t)}{t-y} =\frac{\Pr(Z\le t)\{\mathbb E[Z\mid Z\le t]-y\}}{(t-y)^2}.$$

Hence the ratio decreases before $t_y$, increases after it, and its minimum is $\Pr(Z\le t_y)$. This value is attained by the two-cell information $\mathcal D_y=\sigma\{Z\le t_y\}$: the conditional mean equals $y$ on the lower cell and is strictly greater than $y$ on its complement.

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D]\le y\} =\inf_{y<t\le1}\frac{\mathbb E(t-Z)_+}{t-y} =\Pr(Z\le t_y).$$

For $Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$, the cut is the unique root of $pI_t(a+1,b)=yI_t(a,b)$ and the exact envelope is $I_{t_y}(a,b)$. When $a=N,b=1$, this reduces to $t_y=y/p$ and $(y/p)^N$.

Thus the displayed finite-boundary budget is also sharp over the abstract class $C=(1-\delta)M+\delta R$, $0\le R\le1$, by taking $R=0$. This is an extremal statement over beta contractions, not a claim that every extremizer is realizable by a specified latent calibration mechanism.

The upper tail has an exact dual statement. For $p<y<1$, the upper-truncated mean is continuous and strictly increasing from $p$ to one, so there is a unique $s_y\in(0,y)$ satisfying

$$\mathbb E[Z\mid Z\ge s_y]=y.$$

For every $0\le t<y$, conditional Jensen and $(M-t)_+\ge(y-t)\mathbf1_{\{M\ge y\}}$ give $\Pr(M\ge y)\le\mathbb E(Z-t)_+/(y-t)$.

With $U(t)=\mathbb E(Z-t)_+$, differentiation gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{U(t)}{y-t} =\frac{\Pr(Z\ge t)\{\mathbb E[Z\mid Z\ge t]-y\}}{(y-t)^2}.$$

The ratio decreases before $s_y$ and increases after it. The two-cell information $\sigma\{Z\ge s_y\}$ attains the resulting bound because its upper-cell conditional mean is exactly $y$.

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D]\ge y\} =\inf_{0\le t<y}\frac{\mathbb E(Z-t)_+}{y-t} =\Pr(Z\ge s_y).$$

For $Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$, the cut solves $p\{1-I_s(a+1,b)\}=y\{1-I_s(a,b)\}$ and the envelope is $1-I_{s_y}(a,b)$. When $a=1,b=N$, it reduces to

$$s_y=\frac{y-p}{1-p},\qquad \Pr(Z\ge s_y)=\left(\frac{1-y}{1-p}\right)^N.$$

For $C=(1-\delta)M+\delta R$, $C\le(1-\delta)M+\delta$. Thus for $x>\delta+(1-\delta)p$, put $y=(x-\delta)/(1-\delta)$ and use the upper envelope; it is sharp over the abstract class by taking $R=1$.

The exact two-sided contraction envelope

Fix $0<\ell<p<u<1$, and let $t_\ell$ and $s_u$ be the lower- and upper-tail cuts above. The two one-sided bounds and the union bound give the upper bound below; the following partitions make it exact.

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{M\le\ell\ \text{or}\ M\ge u\} =\min\{1,\Pr(Z\le t_\ell)+\Pr(Z\ge s_u)\}.$$

This upper bound is always attained. If $t_\ell\le s_u$, use the three-cell information generated by $(-\infty,t_\ell]$, $(t_\ell,s_u)$ and $[s_u,\infty)$: its outer conditional means are $\ell,u$, while the middle mean lies strictly between them.

If $t_\ell>s_u$, choose any $c\in[s_u,t_\ell]$ and use $\sigma\{Z\le c\}$. Monotonicity of the truncated means makes the lower-cell mean at most $\ell$ and the upper-cell mean at least $u$, so the two-sided event has probability one.

For $Z\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a,b)$, the exact value is $\min\{1,I_{t_\ell}(a,b)+1-I_{s_u}(a,b)\}$, with the two cuts given by the beta equations above. This is a simultaneous optimizer, not merely a sum of separately valid marginal bounds.

For finite-boundary coverage $C=(1-\delta)M+\delta R$, assume $x_-<(1-\delta)p$ and $x_+>\delta+(1-\delta)p$, and set $\ell=x_-/(1-\delta)$ and $u=(x_+-\delta)/(1-\delta)$. The same envelope is sharp over the abstract class by taking $R=0$ on the lower and middle cells and $R=1$ on the upper cell.

Atoms require a randomized cutoff split

Continuity is needed only to make the optimizing cells deterministic. For an arbitrary law on $[0,1]$, the same lower stop-loss infimum is sharp whenever a minimizing cut $t_*>y$ is used with an independent uniform tie-breaker at the atom $\{Z=t_*\}$.

Write $m_- =\Pr(Z<t_*)$, $z_- =\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_{\{Z<t_*\}}]$ and $h=\Pr(Z=t_*)$. Optimality puts

$$r=\frac{ym_- -z_-}{t_*-y}\in[0,h].$$

Include all $Z<t_*$ and, when $h>0$, a fraction $r/h$ of the cutoff atom; when $h=0$, optimality gives $r=0$. The resulting cell has mean $y$ and mass

$$m_-+r=\frac{\mathbb E(t_*-Z)_+}{t_*-y},$$

so it attains the lower envelope exactly. The upper construction is dual: at a minimizing $s_*<y$, put $m_+=\Pr(Z>s_*)$ and $z_+=\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_{\{Z>s_*\}}]$, then include all $Z>s_*$ and mass $r=[z_+-ym_+]/(y-s_*)$ from the atom at $s_*$.

If the minimizer is the endpoint $t_*=y$ or $s_*=y$, the corresponding one-sided limit gives the result. Thus score atoms do not invalidate either one-sided envelope, but deterministic tie handling can fail to attain it.

Without atom splitting, the gap is explicit

The auxiliary randomization is essential, not merely a proof device. On the canonical two-point space let $Z\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$; its only sub-$\sigma$-fields are the trivial and full ones.

For $0<y<p$, these two choices give $\sup_{\mathcal D\subseteq\sigma(Z)}\Pr\{\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D]\le y\}=1-p$. Splitting the atom at one gives the larger randomized envelope

$$q_-(y)=\frac{1-p}{1-y},\qquad q_-(y)-(1-p)=\frac{(1-p)y}{1-y}>0.$$

Indeed, the optimal lower cell contains the entire zero atom and mass $y(1-p)/(1-y)$ from the one atom. Dually, for $p<y<1$, deterministic information gives upper-tail mass $p$, while splitting the zero atom gives

$$q_+(y)=\frac p y,\qquad q_+(y)-p=\frac{p(1-y)}y>0.$$

Thus the atomic theorem is sharp on an enlarged space carrying an independent tie-breaker, but need not be attainable on a fixed atomic probability space. This distinction is separate from marginal versus feature-conditional conformal coverage.

The fixed-space problem is an exact 0–1 selection

On a canonical finite space with atom masses $w_j$, values $z_j$ and mean $p$, no atom can be split. For $y<p$, every information partition therefore satisfies

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{\mathbb E[Z\mid\mathcal D]\le y\} =\max_{A\subseteq[m]}\left\{\sum_{j\in A}w_j: \sum_{j\in A}w_j(z_j-y)\le0\right\}.$$

Indeed, the union $A$ of all low-mean cells is feasible and has exactly the tail-event mass. Conversely, any feasible $A$ and its complement form a two-cell partition; since the total mean exceeds $y$, the complement mean is greater than $y$.

For $y>p$, the upper-tail formula is the same maximum with the final inequality reversed. Allowing independent atom splitting replaces each indicator $\mathbf1_{\{j\in A\}}$ by a fraction in $[0,1]$, so the sharp randomized stop-loss envelope is exactly the fractional relaxation of this 0–1 problem.

One cutoff atom bounds the randomization gain

The one-sided fractional relaxation is continuous knapsack: order eligible atoms by profit per unit signed cost, breaking ties arbitrarily. It has an optimum that selects every canonical atom either fully or not at all, except possibly one cutoff atom $k$ from which it selects mass $r\in(0,w_k)$.

Deleting that fractional piece preserves feasibility and gives a deterministic event of mass $q_{\rm rand}-r$. Consequently, for either the lower or upper tail,

$$0\le q_{\rm rand}-q_{\rm det}\le r\le w_k\le \max_j w_j.$$

If no atom is fractional, take $r=0$ and the two envelopes agree. The Bernoulli calculations above attain $q_{\rm rand}-q_{\rm det}=r$ for every admissible threshold, so the data-dependent cutoff bound is sharp. Dantzig (1957) supplies the primary continuous-knapsack context.

Exact fixed-space tails already contain SUBSET SUM

Take positive integers $a_1,\ldots,a_m$, a target $0<C<A=\sum_i a_i$, and threshold $y=1/2$. Give one anchor atom mass $w_0=1/2$ and score $z_0=1/2-C/(4A)$; give item $i$ mass $w_i=a_i/(2A)$ and the common score $z_i=3/4$.

The total mean is $p=5/8-C/(8A)>y$. Every nonempty feasible lower-tail selection contains the anchor, and an item set $S$ is feasible exactly when $\sum_{i\in S}a_i\le C$, so

$$q_{\rm det}^-(1/2)=\frac12+\frac1{2A} \max\left\{\sum_{i\in S}a_i:\sum_{i\in S}a_i\le C\right\}.$$

The fractional relaxation always fills the budget exactly and has value $1/2+C/(2A)$. Hence deterministic and randomized envelopes agree if and only if the SUBSET SUM target $C$ is attainable; on this restricted family, deciding equality is NP-complete, and exact fixed-space optimization is weakly NP-hard.

After rational inputs are cleared to integer deviations $d_j$ and masses $c_j$, a signed-knapsack recursion retaining the largest mass at each deviation computes the answer in $O(mD)$ time, where $D=\sum_j|d_j|$. This is a pseudo-polynomial bound, consistent with weak NP-hardness.

Karp (1972) is the primary complexity source for SUBSET SUM; the embedding into conditional-mean tails is the direct construction above.

The one-sided problem is ordinary knapsack and admits an FPTAS

For a general lower threshold $y<p$, every atom with $z_j\le y$ belongs to some optimum: adding it increases mass and weakly decreases the signed constraint. Write

$$W_0=\sum_{z_j\le y}w_j,\qquad B=\sum_{z_j\le y}w_j(y-z_j).$$

The remaining atoms have costs $c_j=w_j(z_j-y)>0$ and profits $v_j=w_j$. Hence the exact deterministic envelope is $q_{\rm det}^-(y)=W_0+V^*$, where $V^*$ is the ordinary $0$–$1$ knapsack optimum $\max\{\sum_{j\in S}v_j:\sum_{j\in S}c_j\le B\}$.

Discard items with $c_j>B$ and let $n$ be the number left. If $n=0$, then $W_0$ is exact; otherwise put $K=\varepsilon v_{\max}/n$ for $0<\varepsilon<1$. Dynamic programming over rounded profits $\lfloor v_j/K\rfloor$ uses $O(n^3/\varepsilon)$ time and $O(n^2/\varepsilon)$ states.

If $\widehat V$ is the selected exact profit, rounding loses less than $nK=\varepsilon v_{\max}\le \varepsilon V^*$. Therefore the returned tail mass obeys the explicit guarantee

$$ (1-\varepsilon)q_{\rm det}^-(y)\le W_0+\widehat V\le q_{\rm det}^-(y). $$

The upper-tail result follows by replacing $Z$ with $1-Z$. Thus weak NP-hardness coexists with a fully polynomial approximation scheme; it is not an approximation-hardness claim. Ibarra and Kim (1975) is the primary FPTAS source.

Fixed-space two-sided bands are a three-way selection

For $\ell<p<u$, the exact deterministic two-sided envelope on the same finite space is

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{M\le\ell\ \text{or}\ M\ge u\} =\max_{A\cap B=\varnothing}\left\{\sum_{j\in A\cup B}w_j: \sum_{j\in A}w_j(z_j-\ell)\le0,\quad \sum_{j\in B}w_j(z_j-u)\ge0\right\}.$$

For any information partition, unite its cells of mean at most $\ell$ into $A$ and its cells of mean at least $u$ into $B$; the sets are disjoint, satisfy the two inequalities, and have exactly the event mass. Conversely, any feasible disjoint pair, together with its remaining atoms, is an information partition whose two-sided event contains $A\cup B$.

Auxiliary randomization replaces the three-way atom assignment by fractions $a_j,b_j\in[0,1]$ with $a_j+b_j\le1$. Its value is the law-level envelope $\min\{1,q_-+q_+\}$ below, so the gap from the displayed integer optimum measures precisely what atom splitting contributes.

Dantzig (1957) supplies historical context for discrete-variable extremum problems and their linear-programming relaxations; the probability-space equality above is proved directly.

Atomic two-sided bands remain sharp

For $0<\ell<p<u<1$, let $q_-$ and $q_+$ be the sharp atomic lower- and upper-tail envelope masses. On the randomized distributional-transform coordinate $V\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$, write $Z=Q(V)$ with nondecreasing quantile $Q$.

The lower optimizer is the prefix $[0,q_-]$ and the upper optimizer is the suffix $[1-q_+,1]$, including fractional endpoint atoms when necessary. If $q_-+q_+\le1$, these cells are disjoint and attain total mass $q_-+q_+$.

If $q_-+q_+>1$, cut instead at $1-q_+$. The suffix has mean $u$, while monotonicity of prefix averages and $1-q_+<q_-$ make the prefix mean at most $\ell$, so every cell is in one of the two tails.

$$\sup_{\mathcal D}\Pr\{M\le\ell\ \text{or}\ M\ge u\} =\min\{1,q_-+q_+\}$$

Thus the simultaneous two-sided formula also survives arbitrary atoms when independent tie randomization is allowed. This is a law-level extremum over randomized contractions, not conditional coverage given observed features.

Randomized contractions are the convex-order lower set

Let $\nu$ be the law of an integrable $Z$, and let $\mathcal C(\nu)$ contain every law of $M=\mathbb E[Z'\mid\mathcal D]$ formed on an arbitrary probability-space extension with $Z'\sim\nu$. Then

$$\mathcal C(\nu)=\{\mu:\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu\}.$$

For the forward inclusion, the tower property gives $\mathbb E[Z'\mid M]=M$, and conditional Jensen gives $\mathbb E\varphi(M)\le\mathbb E\varphi(Z')$ for every integrable convex $\varphi$. Conversely, Strassen's theorem couples any $\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu$ as $(M,Z')$ with $\mathbb E[Z'\mid M]=M$; taking $\mathcal D=\sigma(M)$ realizes that law.

Consequently, the sharp one- and two-sided formulas above optimize over the entire convex-order lower set, not merely over partitions of one convenient representation. This law-level equivalence does not imply attainability on a fixed original space, as the Bernoulli gap shows, or feature-conditional conformal coverage.

At $N=k=9$ and $x=0.8$, the iid beta failure probability is $0.134217728000$, while this latent-model bound is $0.346439416115$. With finite-history boundary weight $\delta=e^{-10}$ it is $0.346581003179$. These are exact contraction envelopes, not a claim of optimality over conformal models.

An exact counterexample to the beta-tail shortcut

Choose a latent regime once, with probability $q=1/50$ for regime zero, and keep it constant for all time. Emit independent scores uniform on $[0,1]$ in regime zero and on $[0,25/18]$ otherwise. This is a bounded stationary binary one-contrast model; its scores are exchangeable but not iid after marginalizing the regime.

Put $r=18/25$, $w=q+(1-q)r^N$ and $a=[q+(1-q)r^{N+1}]/w$. Bayes' formula, using the full calibration panel, shows that coverage conditional on that panel is $C=aQ_N$ if $Q_N\le1$, and $C=rQ_N$ otherwise. Consequently, for $0\le x\le1$,

$$\Pr(C\le x)=w\min\{x/a,1\}^N+(1-q)(x^N-r^N)_+.$$

At $N=k=9$, $a=0.798919119391<0.8$, so

$$\Pr(C\le0.8)=q+(1-q)0.8^9 =\frac{14798181}{97656250}=0.151533373440 >0.8^9=0.134217728000.$$

The marginal mean remains exactly $0.9$. Its conditional-coverage variance is $0.007260502147$, smaller than the iid value $0.008181818182$, despite the larger failure probability at $0.8$. This does not contradict the beta law for a fixed iid score distribution.

Application to exponential ages and finite history

Let $X_t$ be a bounded, jointly measurable, strictly stationary real signal with mean zero and fixed genuine continuous emission CDFs $\bar F+X_tH$. Assume emissions independent conditional on the full signal and times, and independently draw labeled ages iid $\operatorname{Exp}(\beta)$ truncated to $[0,L]$. For $\delta=e^{-\beta L}$, define

$$U_t=\frac{\beta}{1-\delta}\int_0^L e^{-\beta a}X_{t-a}\,da.$$

Its absolutely continuous drift is proportional to $X_t-\delta X_{t-L}-(1-\delta)U_t$. For every continuously differentiable $\varphi$ and $T>0$, the integrated chain rule, bounded Fubini and joint stationarity give

$$0=\mathbb E[\varphi(U_T)-\varphi(U_0)] =\frac{\beta T}{1-\delta}\mathbb E[\varphi'(U_0) \{X_0-\delta X_{-L}-(1-\delta)U_0\}].$$

Every continuous function on the compact range of $U_0$ is such a derivative, so

$$\mathbb E[X_0\mid U_0]=(1-\delta)U_0+\delta\mathbb E[X_{-L}\mid U_0].$$

Given the signal, the calibration joint law depends only on $U_0$, with CDF $G_U=\bar F+U_0H$. The test CDF given $U_0$ is $(1-\delta)G_U+\delta B_U$, where $B_U=\bar F+\mathbb E[X_{-L}\mid U_0]H$ is a genuine boundary mixture, proving the abstract hypotheses above.

For untruncated ages, use $U_t=\beta\int_0^\infty e^{-\beta a}X_{t-a}da$. The drift is $\beta(X_t-U_t)$, the same chain-rule argument gives $\mathbb E[X_0\mid U_0]=U_0$, and $\delta=0$. Neither argument requires mixing, Markov dynamics or a stationary filter density.

These bounds concern coverage conditional on the specified score-only information, not on realized times, chronological order, the full observed history, features or current regime. They do not cover adaptive rank selection, panel-fitted scores, overlapping path emissions or repeated-panel anytime guarantees.

Certificate and classical context

The certificate checks 1,026 exact rational stop-loss/tail comparisons and 54 mean/variance cases for nested uniform emission laws. It also checks 210 sharp one-sided beta contractions, 315 continuous and 54 atomic two-sided partition witnesses, 30 exact Bernoulli no-split gaps, 38 cutoff-atom gap bounds, 18 signed dynamic programs, 169 SUBSET SUM reductions, 24 ordinary-knapsack reductions, 96 FPTAS guarantees, 54 fixed-space two-sided optima, 66 finite-law convex-order witnesses and 162 exact latent variance decompositions; the full crossover regression independently checks the beta-binomial benchmark.
