The likelihood of a switching short rate

A short rate observed at discrete times, with a level and volatility switched by a hidden regime much faster than the sampling: the first-order stationary-start transition density, what it identifies, and the cone-corrected test for switching it implies.

The model

The short rate follows a Vasicek model whose mean level and variance depend on a hidden Markov chain $y_t$:

$$dx_t = \kappa\,\big(\theta_{y_t} - x_t\big)\,dt + \sigma_{y_t}\,dW_t .$$

The rate is observed every $\Delta$, giving $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n$. The chain is not observed. It switches many times between observations, so $\lambda\Delta \gg 1$ for switching rates $\lambda$.

There are three time scales: the regimes last about $1/\lambda$, observations come every $\Delta$, and the rate reverts over $1/\kappa$.

Parameters for the checks: $\kappa = 0.5$, $\theta = (0.08,\ 0.02)$, $\sigma^2 = (0.0009,\ 0.0001)$, monthly observations $\Delta = 1/12$.

The quantity

The calculation below gives the transition density with the regime drawn from its stationary law $\pi$ at the start of each interval and summed at the end. Its sum

$$\ell_C = \sum_{k} \log p_\pi\big(x_{k+1} \mid x_k\big)$$

is a stationary-start transition composite likelihood. The distinction matters: after the regime is hidden, $x_t$ alone is generally not Markov, because the past observations filter the current regime. Fast mixing makes endpoint regime memory exponentially small, but that fact by itself does not make the displayed product the exact observed-data likelihood. A full likelihood propagates the hidden-state filter. The fixed-panel theorem below shows when the two likelihoods nevertheless agree through first order; it does not make the observations exactly Markov or give a uniform long-panel equivalence.

When the composite likelihood is first-order exact

Write $Q=mQ_0$ and let $K_m(x,x')$ be the matrix transition density: its $(i,j)$ entry is the joint density of $x'$ and ending regime $j$, given $x$ and starting regime $i$. For fixed $\Delta>0$, suppose uniformly on a compact observation set that

$$K_m=p_0\,\mathbf1\pi^\top+m^{-1}K_1+O(m^{-2}), \qquad K_m\mathbf1=p_0\mathbf1+m^{-1}h+O(m^{-2}),$$

with $p_0$ bounded away from zero. This is the regime-resolved version of the fast-switching expansion; the exponentially small endpoint transient is absorbed by the remainder.

If the incoming filter is $\nu_k=\pi+m^{-1}r_k+O(m^{-2})$, where $r_k\mathbf1=0$, its predictive density differs from the stationary-start density only at second order:

$$\nu_kK_m\mathbf1-\pi K_m\mathbf1 =m^{-1}r_k(p_0\mathbf1)+O(m^{-2})=O(m^{-2}).$$

Moreover the Bayes update $\nu_{k+1}=\nu_kK_m/(\nu_kK_m\mathbf1)$ is again $\pi+O(m^{-1})$. Induction therefore gives, for a fixed panel of $N$ transitions started from $\pi$,

$$\ell_F-\ell_C=O(Nm^{-2}),$$

where $\ell_F$ is the exact filtered log likelihood. An arbitrary initial regime prior changes the first predictive density by $O(m^{-1})$; after that observation the filter is $\pi+O(m^{-1})$, so the remaining gap is $O(Nm^{-2})$. For a growing panel the displayed bound tends to zero only if $N/m^2\to0$, and it is $o(m^{-1})$ only if $N/m\to0$. No long-panel equivalence is asserted.

The second-order likelihood coefficient is explicit. For transition $k$, write

$$K_{m,k}=p_k\mathbf1\pi^\top+m^{-1}K_{1,k}+O(m^{-2}),\qquad h_k=K_{1,k}\mathbf1.$$

For a stationary start set $r_0=0$ and propagate

$$r_{k+1}=\frac{\pi K_{1,k}-(\pi h_k)\pi}{p_k}.$$

The incoming $r_k$ drops out of this recursion because $r_k\mathbf1=0$: the leading kernel maps every starting regime to the same stationary ending law. Consequently, for fixed $N$ and a uniform kernel expansion,

$$\ell_F-\ell_C=\frac1{m^2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{r_kh_k}{p_k}+O(Nm^{-3}).$$

For an arbitrary initial prior $\nu$, the first coefficient is $(\nu-\pi)h_0/p_0$, while the filter entering the second interval has $r_1=\{\nu K_{1,0}-(\nu h_0)\pi\}/p_0$. After the next update, its first-order correction no longer depends on $\nu$. Thus the theorem identifies not just the rates but the pathwise coefficients and the exact point at which initial-prior memory disappears at each order.

Averaging

When the chain switches infinitely fast the rate is Vasicek with averaged parameters, observed as an AR(1):

$$x_{k+1} = \bar\theta + (x_k - \bar\theta)\,e^{-\kappa\Delta} + \sqrt v\,Z_k, \qquad v = \bar s\,\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa\Delta}}{2\kappa}, \qquad Z_k \sim N(0, 1),$$

with $\bar\theta = \pi\cdot\theta$ and $\bar s = \pi\cdot\sigma^2$. The transition density is the Gaussian $f(x_{k+1})$ with mean $m_k = \bar\theta + (x_k - \bar\theta)e^{-\kappa\Delta}$ and variance $v$.

Reduction to a linear system

The Laplace transform of the next observation is exponential-affine. Try $\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-c\,x_{k+1}} \mid x_k,\ y\big] = e^{-B(\Delta)\,x_k}\,a_y(\Delta)$. With no discounting the coefficient solves $B' = -\kappa B$ from $B(0) = c$, so

$$B(t) = c\,e^{-\kappa t},$$

the same in every regime. What remains is

$$a' = \big(Q + \operatorname{diag} g\big)\,a, \qquad a(0) = \mathbf 1, \qquad g_i(t) = -\kappa\,\theta_i\,B(t) + \tfrac12\,\sigma_i^2\,B(t)^2 .$$

The first-order transition composite likelihood

Let $u = \theta - \bar\theta$ and $w = \sigma^2 - \bar s$ be the fluctuations of level and variance across regimes. The first-order correction to $\log(\pi\cdot a)$ is the Green–Kubo integral of the fluctuating forcing, as on the yield-curve page:

$$\int_0^\Delta \mathcal K\big(\tilde g, \tilde g\big)\,dt, \qquad \tilde g = -\kappa\,u\,B + \tfrac12\,w\,B^2, \qquad \mathcal K(f, h) = -\pi\cdot\big(f\,Q^{\#}h\big).$$

Since $B = c\,e^{-\kappa t}$, this is a polynomial in $c$:

$$\int_0^\Delta \mathcal K(\tilde g, \tilde g)\,dt = A_2\,c^2 + A_3\,c^3 + A_4\,c^4,$$ $$\begin{aligned} A_2 &= \kappa^2\,\mathcal K(u, u)\,\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa\Delta}}{2\kappa}, \\ A_3 &= -\kappa\,\mathcal K_{\mathrm{sym}}(u, w)\,\frac{1 - e^{-3\kappa\Delta}}{3\kappa}, \\ A_4 &= \tfrac14\,\mathcal K(w, w)\,\frac{1 - e^{-4\kappa\Delta}}{4\kappa}. \end{aligned}$$

A factor $c^n$ on the transform is the $n$-th derivative of the Gaussian density, and that derivative is a Hermite polynomial times the density:

$$f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\,v^{-n/2}\,\mathrm{He}_n(z)\,f(x), \qquad z = \frac{x - m_k}{\sqrt v}.$$

So the transition density and the stationary-start transition composite likelihood are, to first order,

$$\begin{aligned} p(x_{k+1} \mid x_k) &= f(x_{k+1})\Big(1 + \frac{A_2}{v}\,\mathrm{He}_2(z_k) - \frac{A_3}{v^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{He}_3(z_k) + \frac{A_4}{v^2}\,\mathrm{He}_4(z_k)\Big), \\ \ell_C &= \sum_k \Big(\log f(x_{k+1}) + \frac{A_2}{v}\,\mathrm{He}_2(z_k) - \frac{A_3}{v^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{He}_3(z_k) + \frac{A_4}{v^2}\,\mathrm{He}_4(z_k)\Big), \end{aligned}$$ $$\mathrm{He}_2(z) = z^2 - 1, \qquad \mathrm{He}_3(z) = z^3 - 3z, \qquad \mathrm{He}_4(z) = z^4 - 6z^2 + 3 .$$

The terms have plain meanings. $A_2$ widens the density, as extra variance would.

$A_3$ skews it, through the covariance between a regime's level and its volatility. $A_4$ fattens its tails, through the variance of the volatility.

What the stationary-start transition density identifies

  • To first order the chain enters only through the $2\times2$ Green–Kubo matrix of $(u, w)$, the same matrix that the yield curve sees. Different chains with the same averages and matrix have the same likelihood to first order.
  • The $\mathrm{He}_2$ term is indistinguishable from a larger variance: the data see $v + 2A_2$, which is proportional to $\bar s + 2\kappa^2\mathcal K(u, u)$. The yield curve sees the same combination, so neither the time series nor the cross section separates the average variance from the level's Green–Kubo term.
  • $A_3$ and $A_4$ are identified, and they give $\mathcal K_{\mathrm{sym}}(u, w)$ and $\mathcal K(w, w)$.
  • The matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A_3^2 \le 4A_2A_4$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $e^{-2\kappa t}$ and $e^{-4\kappa t}$.

The cone-corrected test for switching

With no switching the matrix is zero. The scores for $A_3$ and $A_4$ at zero are sums of Hermite polynomials of the AR(1) residuals, whose sample means are the residual skewness and excess kurtosis:

$$\frac{\partial\ell_C}{\partial A_3} = -\frac{1}{v^{3/2}}\sum_k \mathrm{He}_3(z_k), \qquad \frac{\partial\ell_C}{\partial A_4} = \frac{1}{v^2}\sum_k \mathrm{He}_4(z_k).$$

Since $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_3^2] = 6$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_4^2] = 24$ under the null, put

$$Z_3=\sqrt{n}\,\hat S/\sqrt6,\qquad Z_4=\sqrt{n}\,\hat K/\sqrt{24}.$$

The ordinary Jarque–Bera statistic is $Z_3^2+Z_4^2$; it tests both positive and negative excess kurtosis. That is not the score statistic for this model.

Here $A_4\geq0$. After the variance correction $A_2$ is profiled into the Gaussian variance, the closure of the local alternative cone in the skewness–kurtosis coordinates is $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R_+$. Indeed, for every $A_4>0$ and either sign of $A_3$, the positive-semidefinite constraint $A_3^2\leq4A_2A_4$ can be met by choosing $A_2$ large enough, while $A_2$ is absorbed by the interior variance nuisance parameter. Projecting the Gaussian score onto that cone gives

$$\mathrm{LM}_+=Z_3^2+(Z_4^+)^2 =n\left\{\frac{\hat S^2}{6}+\frac{\max(\hat K,0)^2}{24}\right\}.$$

Thus negative sample excess kurtosis contributes zero, not its square. Under the Gaussian null, with regular estimation of the Gaussian AR parameters, Hermite orthogonality makes the third- and fourth-order scores asymptotically independent standard normals and orthogonal to the nuisance scores. Therefore

$$\mathrm{LM}_+\ \Longrightarrow\ \tfrac12\chi_1^2+\tfrac12\chi_2^2.$$

This is a chi-bar-square law, with 95% critical value $5.138381$. It is the projection geometry of boundary tests described by Self and Liang (1987) and Shapiro (1988). Ordinary Jarque–Bera remains the right omnibus score test for unrestricted nonnormality; $\mathrm{LM}_+$ is the directional test implied by the fast-switching variance-mixture model.

General Fisher covariance

The independent Hermite coordinates are convenient, but not essential. Let $Z=(Z_1,Z_2)$ be the unconstrained Gaussian local estimator after profiling the regular nuisance parameters, with positive-definite covariance $\Sigma$, where the local cone is $\{h:h_2\geq0\}$. Equivalently, $\Sigma^{-1}$ is the Fisher metric in these coordinates. Put

$$V=\frac{Z_2}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}}},\qquad U=\frac{Z_1-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}Z_2} {\sqrt{\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22}}}.$$

Under the null, $U$ and $V$ are independent standard normals. The metric projection onto the half-space gives

$$\mathrm{LM}_{\Sigma}=U^2+\max(V,0)^2 =\begin{cases} Z^\top\Sigma^{-1}Z,& Z_2\geq0,\\[2pt] \displaystyle\frac{(Z_1-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}Z_2)^2} {\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22}},& Z_2<0. \end{cases}$$

Consequently the null law remains $\tfrac12\chi_1^2+\tfrac12\chi_2^2$ for every nonsingular $\Sigma$. What is invariant is the Fisher-metric projection, not marginal standardization followed by coordinatewise clipping. The latter generally has the wrong null law when the two coordinates are correlated. The simple $Z_3^2+(Z_4^+)^2$ formula above is the special case $\Sigma=I$ supplied by Hermite orthogonality.

Local alternatives and power

The same projection gives an exact local-asymptotic power calculation. Suppose a triangular sequence of alternatives, after the same nuisance-parameter profiling, satisfies

$$\left(Z_3,Z_4\right)\ \Longrightarrow\ \left(G_3+\delta_3,G_4+\delta_4\right),\qquad G_3,G_4\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1),\qquad \delta_4\geq0.$$

Then, by continuous mapping,

$$\mathrm{LM}_+\ \Longrightarrow\ (G_3+\delta_3)^2+\max(G_4+\delta_4,0)^2.$$

This is generally not a noncentral chi-bar-square with fixed one-half weights: when $\delta_4>0$, the probability of landing on the boundary face is $\Phi(-\delta_4)$ rather than $1/2$. For a critical value $c$, put $r=\sqrt c$ and $H_{\delta}(a)=\Pr\{|G+\delta|>a\}=\Phi(-a-\delta)+1-\Phi(a-\delta)$. The limiting power is the one-dimensional formula

$$\beta(\delta_3,\delta_4;c)= \Phi(-\delta_4)H_{\delta_3}(r) +\int_0^r H_{\delta_3}\!\left(\sqrt{c-y^2}\right)\phi(y-\delta_4)\,dy +1-\Phi(r-\delta_4).$$

At $(\delta_3,\delta_4)=(0,0)$ this reduces exactly to the chi-bar-square null law. The statement concerns local power in the two-score Gaussian limit. It does not assert finite-sample optimality, a rate relating $n$ to the switching speed, or equivalence to the full filtered-likelihood test.

For a general covariance and raw local mean $\delta=(\delta_1,\delta_2)$, the same formula applies after the canonical transformation

$$\eta_1=\frac{\delta_1-\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\delta_2} {\sqrt{\Sigma_{11}-\Sigma_{12}^2/\Sigma_{22}}},\qquad \eta_2=\frac{\delta_2}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{22}}},$$

with $(\delta_3,\delta_4)$ in $\beta$ replaced by $(\eta_1,\eta_2)$. In particular, the one-sided condition is preserved: $\delta_2\geq0$ if and only if $\eta_2\geq0$.

This is an information-matrix-type construction, related to the full-likelihood test that Carrasco, Hu and Ploberger (2014) show to be optimal for Markov switching, building on White (1982) and Chesher (1984). Because the density used here is a stationary-start composite likelihood, no corresponding full-data optimality claim is made. What the continuous-time model adds is the structure of the coefficients: they are Green–Kubo integrals with explicit time factors, bound by the inequality above.

Tests for switching are usually non-standard because the switching parameters are not identified under the null (Hansen 1992; Garcia 1998; Cho and White 2007). Here the first-order working parameters are Green–Kubo combinations: $A_3$ and $A_4$ are identified by the transition shape, while $A_2$ is profiled with the Gaussian variance. The null remains the vertex of a positive-semidefinite cone, so the kurtosis direction is one-sided.

Unconstrained moment diagnostics follow from the same expansion:

$$\hat A_3 = -\frac{\hat S\,v^{3/2}}{6}, \qquad \hat A_4 = \frac{\hat K\,v^2}{24} .$$

A negative $\hat A_4$ diagnoses a direction outside the model; it is not a feasible switching estimate. A constrained estimator should optimize the full positive-semidefinite Green–Kubo matrix and the Gaussian variance jointly, rather than clipping these two formulas separately.

Scope

The expansion needs many regime changes between observations. When regimes last longer than the sampling interval, the first-order cancellation above is unavailable and the exact likelihood needs the filter of Hamilton (1989). Even in the fast-switching limit, the equivalence theorem is pointwise on compact observation sets with predictive densities bounded away from zero; it is not a tail-uniform likelihood theorem.

A switching variance alone gives the classic normal variance mixture of Clark (1973). For closed-form likelihood expansions of diffusions without regimes see Aït-Sahalia (2002).
