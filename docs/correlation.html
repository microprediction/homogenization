Correlation that switches with the market

Two assets whose correlation jumps between a calm regime and a crisis regime: the correlation smile it puts into exchange and spread options, and the co-skewness it puts into returns when correlation rises in down markets.

Correlations are low in calm markets and high in crises. When the regimes switch fast compared with the horizon, the first-order rule turns this into two closed forms: a correlation volga in option prices, and a third cross-cumulant in the joint law of returns.

The model

Two log prices $x_1, x_2$ follow Black–Scholes dynamics under the pricing measure, with fixed volatilities and a correlation set by a hidden regime $y_t$:

$$dx_j = \big(r - \tfrac12\sigma_j^2\big)\,dt + \sigma_j\,dW_j, \qquad d\langle W_1, W_2\rangle_t = \rho_{y_t}\,dt .$$

The regime is calm or crisis. It moves from calm to crisis at rate $a$ and back at rate $b$, and starts from its stationary law because it cannot be observed.

There are three time scales. A crisis lasts $1/b$ on average, a calm spell lasts $1/a$, and the options run for time $T$. The expansion is in the ratio of the first two to the third, and the checks speed the chain up by a factor $m$.

Parameters: $\rho = (0.2,\ 0.8)$ in (calm, crisis); $a = 4/3$ and $b = 4$ per year, so crises last three months and take a quarter of the time; $\sigma_1 = 0.30$, $\sigma_2 = 0.20$, $S_1 = S_2 = 100$, $r = 2\%$, $T = 1$.

The quantity

Two payoffs that depend on the correlation. The exchange option and the spread option pay

$$\big(S_1(T) - q\,S_2(T)\big)^+ \qquad\text{and}\qquad \big(S_1(T) - S_2(T) - K\big)^+ .$$

Each price is quoted as an implied correlation: the constant $\rho$ that reproduces it in the two-asset Black–Scholes model. A model with one correlation gives the same implied correlation at every strike.

Averaging

The stationary law puts weight $b/(a + b) = 0.75$ on calm and $a/(a + b) = 0.25$ on crisis. When the chain switches infinitely fast the model is two-asset Black–Scholes with the averaged correlation

$$\bar\rho = \frac{b\,\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} + a\,\rho_{\mathrm{crisis}}}{a + b} = 0.35 .$$

The averaged model has a flat implied correlation at $0.35$.

Reduction to a mixture over correlation

The pricing operator in regime $y$ is the averaged operator plus the correlation fluctuation times one operator:

$$\mathcal L_y = \bar{\mathcal L} + (\rho_y - \bar\rho)\,A, \qquad A = \sigma_1\sigma_2\,\partial_{x_1}\partial_{x_2} .$$

$A$ has constant coefficients, so it commutes with $\bar{\mathcal L}$. Along any path of the chain the operators can be collected, and the log prices at $T$ are Gaussian with correlation equal to the time average

$$\hat\rho_T = \frac1T\int_0^T \rho_{y_s}\,ds .$$

So the price is a mixture of constant-correlation prices, the correlation analogue of the mixing formula of Hull and White (1987):

$$C = \mathbb E\big[\,C_{\rho}\big|_{\rho = \hat\rho_T}\big].$$

For two regimes the law of $\hat\rho_T$ is explicit. With $v$ the time spent in crisis and $u = T - v$, a path that starts calm has density

$$e^{-au - bv}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{ab\,u}{v}}\;I_1\big(2\sqrt{ab\,uv}\big) + a\,I_0\big(2\sqrt{ab\,uv}\big)\Big)$$

on $0 < v < T$, plus an atom $e^{-aT}$ at $v = 0$. The two terms count paths that end calm and end in crisis.

A path that starts in crisis has the same form with $a \leftrightarrow b$ and $u \leftrightarrow v$. Quadrature over this law gives the numerical solution used in the checks.

The first-order rule

Differentiating a constant-correlation price in $\rho$ brings down the operator that $\rho$ multiplies, for the whole time $T$:

$$\frac{\partial C_\rho}{\partial\rho} = T\,A\,C_\rho, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 C_\rho}{\partial\rho^2} = T^2 A^2 C_\rho .$$

The rule adds the correction $K_{\rho\rho}A^2$ to the averaged operator. Because $A$ commutes with $\bar{\mathcal L}$, the Duhamel integral is $T$ times $K_{\rho\rho}A^2$ applied to the averaged price, and $A^2$ is $T^{-2}\partial_\rho^2$:

$$C \;\approx\; C_{\bar\rho} + \frac{K_{\rho\rho}}{T}\,\frac{\partial^2 C_\rho}{\partial\rho^2}\Big|_{\rho = \bar\rho},$$ $$\begin{aligned} K_{\rho\rho} &= \int_0^\infty \operatorname{Cov}_\pi\big(\rho_{y_0},\,\rho_{y_\tau}\big)\,d\tau = \frac{ab\,(\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} - \rho_{\mathrm{crisis}})^2}{(a + b)^3} . \end{aligned}$$

The correction is half the variance of $\hat\rho_T$, which is $2K_{\rho\rho}/T$ to first order, times the convexity of the price in correlation. This convexity is a correlation volga. It changes sign across strikes, so no single correlation absorbs it, and the implied correlation varies with the strike.

Here $K_{\rho\rho} = 0.01266$. Over one year $\hat\rho_T$ has a standard deviation of $0.14$, against $0.16$ from $2K_{\rho\rho}/T$.

The exchange option written out

Take $S_2$ as numeraire. The payoff becomes $S_2(T)\,(Z_T - q)^+$ with $Z = S_1/S_2$, and under the new measure $Z$ is a martingale with variance rate

$$s_y = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho_y\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 .$$

The change of measure involves only $W_2$ and the fixed $\sigma_2$, so it leaves the chain alone. The exchange option is therefore exactly the one-dimensional problem of a switching variance, the smile of the general rule, with switched variance $s$ and Green–Kubo coefficient

$$K_{ss} = 4\,\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\,K_{\rho\rho} .$$

The price is Margrabe's formula at the averaged exchange variance plus a volga term:

$$C \;\approx\; S_1\,N(d_1) - q\,S_2\,N(d_2) \;+\; T\,K_{ss}\,\frac{S_1\,\varphi(d_1)\,(d_1 d_2 - 1)}{4\,\bar V^{3/2}},$$ $$\begin{aligned} d_1 &= \frac{\log\big(S_1/(qS_2)\big) + \bar V/2}{\sqrt{\bar V}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sqrt{\bar V}, \\ \bar V &= \bar s\,T, \qquad \bar s = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\bar\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2, \\ \bar\rho &= \frac{b\,\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} + a\,\rho_{\mathrm{crisis}}}{a + b}, \\ K_{ss} &= 4\,\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\,K_{\rho\rho}, \qquad K_{\rho\rho} = \frac{ab\,(\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} - \rho_{\mathrm{crisis}})^2}{(a + b)^3}, \\ \varphi(d) &= e^{-d^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}, \qquad N \text{ the standard normal distribution function.} \end{aligned}$$

The interest rate drops out, as it does in Margrabe's formula.

The correlation smile in closed form

To first order the implied correlation moves from $\bar\rho$ by the correction divided by the correlation vega:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{imp}} \approx \bar\rho + \frac{K_{\rho\rho}}{T}\,\frac{\partial_\rho^2 C}{\partial_\rho C} .$$

For the exchange option $\partial_\rho = -2\sigma_1\sigma_2 T\,\partial_V$. The ratio of volga to vega in total variance is $(d_1 d_2 - 1)/(2\bar V)$, and $d_1 d_2 = k^2/\bar V - \bar V/4$ at log-moneyness $k$. Together:

$$\rho_{\mathrm{imp}}(k) \;\approx\; \bar\rho + \frac{K_{\rho\rho}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\bar V}\Big(1 + \frac{\bar V}{4} - \frac{k^2}{\bar V}\Big),$$ $$\begin{aligned} k &= \log\frac{S_1}{q\,S_2}, \\ \bar V &= \big(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\bar\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\big)\,T, \\ \bar\rho &= \frac{b\,\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} + a\,\rho_{\mathrm{crisis}}}{a + b}, \qquad K_{\rho\rho} = \frac{ab\,(\rho_{\mathrm{calm}} - \rho_{\mathrm{crisis}})^2}{(a + b)^3} . \end{aligned}$$

The implied correlation is a downward parabola in log-moneyness. It peaks at the money, above the average correlation, and falls below $\bar\rho$ once $k^2 > \bar V(1 + \bar V/4)$.

The shape is the volatility smile turned over. A higher correlation lowers the exchange variance, so the variance smile, which rises in the wings, becomes a correlation frown.

A worked example

With the parameters above, $\bar s = 0.088$, $\bar V = 0.088$, $K_{\rho\rho} = 0.012656$ and $K_{ss} = 1.8225\times10^{-4}$. The parabola peaks at $0.35 + 0.00882 = 0.3588$ and crosses $\bar\rho$ at $|k| = 0.300$, that is at $q = 0.74$ and $q = 1.35$.

The spread option

The spread option has no closed form at a fixed correlation, so $C_\rho$ comes from one-dimensional quadrature. Conditioning on the second asset leaves a Black–Scholes price in the first:

$$C_\rho = e^{-rT}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(z)\,\Big(F(z)\,N\big(d_1(z)\big) - \big(S_2(z) + K\big)\,N\big(d_2(z)\big)\Big)\,dz,$$ $$\begin{aligned} S_2(z) &= S_2\,e^{(r - \sigma_2^2/2)T + \sigma_2\sqrt T\,z}, \qquad F(z) = S_1\,e^{rT - \rho^2\sigma_1^2T/2 + \rho\,\sigma_1\sqrt T\,z}, \\ d_{1,2}(z) &= \frac{\log\big(F(z)/(S_2(z) + K)\big) \pm v/2}{\sqrt v}, \qquad v = \sigma_1^2\,(1 - \rho^2)\,T, \end{aligned}$$

with the bracket replaced by $F(z) - S_2(z) - K$ where $S_2(z) + K \le 0$. The integral is evaluated with 120 Gauss–Hermite nodes, and $\partial_\rho^2 C$ by a five-point difference in $\rho$. At $K = 0$ it reproduces Margrabe's formula to $10^{-12}$.

Correlation that rises in down markets

Under the physical measure the crisis regime also has lower drifts and higher volatilities. Let every entry of the drift and of the covariance switch:

$$dx_j = \mu_{j,y_t}\,dt + \sigma_{j,y_t}\,dW_j, \qquad d\langle W_1, W_2\rangle_t = \rho_{y_t}\,dt .$$

Write the covariance entries as $c_{11} = \sigma_1^2$, $c_{22} = \sigma_2^2$ and $c_{12} = \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2$, each a function of the regime. Here $\mu_j$ is the drift of the log price.

Parameters, in (calm, crisis): $\mu_1 = (0.10,\ -0.20)$, $\mu_2 = (0.08,\ -0.25)$, $\sigma_1 = (0.15,\ 0.30)$, $\sigma_2 = (0.12,\ 0.25)$, $\rho = (0.2,\ 0.8)$; the same chain; horizon $t = 1$.

The quantity

The joint cumulants of the log returns $X = x_t - x_0$, read from the cumulant generating function

$$\Lambda(\theta) = \log\mathbb E\,e^{\theta_1 X_1 + \theta_2 X_2} = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\kappa_{ij}}{i!\,j!}\,\theta_1^i\theta_2^j,$$

with the index written out, so that $\kappa_{21}$ is $\kappa_{112}$ and $\kappa_{11}$ is $\kappa_{12}$. The third cumulant $\kappa_{112} = \mathbb E\big[(X_1 - \mathbb E X_1)^2(X_2 - \mathbb E X_2)\big]$ is the co-skewness. It is negative when the second asset does badly at times when the first moves a lot.

Reduction

Given the regime path the returns are Gaussian, so

$$\mathbb E\,e^{\theta\cdot X} = \pi\cdot\exp\big(t\,(Q + \operatorname{diag} g(\theta))\big)\,\mathbf 1, \qquad g_y(\theta) = \theta\cdot\mu_y + \tfrac12\,\theta^{\top}c_y\,\theta .$$

This is the engine system with a constant forcing. All operators are multiplications, so they commute and only the symmetric part of the Green–Kubo matrix enters.

The first-order rule

With a stationary start the rule gives the averaged exponent plus the Green–Kubo variance of the fluctuating forcing $\tilde g$:

$$\Lambda(\theta) \approx t\,\bar g(\theta) + t\,K(\tilde g, \tilde g), \qquad K(f, h) = -\pi\cdot\big(f\,Q^{\#}h\big) .$$

Expanding $K(\tilde g, \tilde g)$ in $\theta$ gives three pieces:

$$K(\tilde g, \tilde g) = K(\theta\cdot\mu,\ \theta\cdot\mu) + K\big(\theta\cdot\mu,\ \theta^{\top}c\,\theta\big) + \tfrac14\,K\big(\theta^{\top}c\,\theta,\ \theta^{\top}c\,\theta\big).$$

The first is quadratic in $\theta$ and adds to the covariance. The second is cubic, and it is the only source of third cumulants at first order. The third is quartic and fattens the tails.

The coefficient of $\theta_1^2\theta_2$ in the cubic piece is $2K(\mu_1, c_{12}) + K(\mu_2, c_{11})$, so

$$\kappa_{112} \approx 2t\,\big[\,2K(\mu_1, c_{12}) + K(\mu_2, c_{11})\,\big], \qquad \kappa_{122} \approx 2t\,\big[\,2K(\mu_2, c_{12}) + K(\mu_1, c_{22})\,\big].$$

For a portfolio with weights $w$ the same step gives the third cumulant

$$\kappa_3(w) \approx 6t\,K\big(w\cdot\mu,\ w^{\top}c\,w\big).$$

A regime whose mean is low when its covariance is high makes $K(\mu, c) < 0$ and a negative third cumulant. The term $2w_1w_2\,c_{12}$ inside $w^{\top}c\,w$ is the part carried by the correlation.

Written out for two regimes

For the two-state chain $K(f, h) = ab\,\Delta f\,\Delta h/(a + b)^3$, with $\Delta$ the calm value minus the crisis value. Every cumulant through third order is then:

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa_{12} &\approx t\,\bar c_{12} + 2t\,G\,\Delta\mu_1\,\Delta\mu_2, \\ \kappa_{112} &\approx 2t\,G\,\big(2\,\Delta\mu_1\,\Delta c_{12} + \Delta\mu_2\,\Delta c_{11}\big), \\ \kappa_{122} &\approx 2t\,G\,\big(2\,\Delta\mu_2\,\Delta c_{12} + \Delta\mu_1\,\Delta c_{22}\big), \\ \kappa_{111} &\approx 6t\,G\,\Delta\mu_1\,\Delta c_{11}, \qquad \kappa_{222} \approx 6t\,G\,\Delta\mu_2\,\Delta c_{22}, \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} G &= \frac{ab}{(a + b)^3}, \\ \Delta\mu_j &= \mu_{j,\mathrm{calm}} - \mu_{j,\mathrm{crisis}}, \\ \Delta c_{jj} &= \sigma_{j,\mathrm{calm}}^2 - \sigma_{j,\mathrm{crisis}}^2, \\ \Delta c_{12} &= \rho_{\mathrm{calm}}\,\sigma_{1,\mathrm{calm}}\sigma_{2,\mathrm{calm}} - \rho_{\mathrm{crisis}}\,\sigma_{1,\mathrm{crisis}}\sigma_{2,\mathrm{crisis}}, \\ \bar c_{12} &= \frac{b\,\rho_{\mathrm{calm}}\,\sigma_{1,\mathrm{calm}}\sigma_{2,\mathrm{calm}} + a\,\rho_{\mathrm{crisis}}\,\sigma_{1,\mathrm{crisis}}\sigma_{2,\mathrm{crisis}}}{a + b} . \end{aligned}$$

The variances $\kappa_{11}$ and $\kappa_{22}$ follow the pattern of $\kappa_{12}$. With a bear regime, $\Delta\mu_j > 0$ and $\Delta c < 0$, and all third cumulants are negative.

A worked example

With the parameters above, $G = 0.035156$, $\Delta\mu = (0.30,\ 0.33)$, $\Delta c_{11} = -0.0675$, $\Delta c_{22} = -0.0481$ and $\Delta c_{12} = 0.0036 - 0.0600 = -0.0564$. So

$$\kappa_{112} \approx 2 \times 0.035156 \times \big(2 \times 0.30 \times (-0.0564) + 0.33 \times (-0.0675)\big) = -3.946\times10^{-3},$$

and $\kappa_{122} \approx -3.632\times10^{-3}$. Of $\kappa_{112}$, the correlation switch through $\Delta c_{12}$ supplies $60\%$ and the volatility of the second asset the rest.

For the equal-weight portfolio, $\kappa_3 \approx -3.79\times10^{-3}$, half of it from the correlation switch. Its skewness over one year is $-0.64$ in the numerical solution. The averaged model is Gaussian, with no skewness at all.

The same simulated returns show the asymmetry that the empirical literature measures.

The correlation of the two returns, given that both lie below their means, is $0.563$. Given that both lie above, it is $0.254$. A Gaussian with the same overall correlation, $0.612$, gives $0.367$ on both sides.

Prior art

That correlations rise in falling markets is well documented.

Longin and Solnik (2001) find, with extreme-value methods, that international equity correlation rises in bear markets but not in bull markets, and that it is tied to the trend rather than to volatility. Ang and Chen (2002) find much larger downside than upside exceedance correlations for US stocks, and that regime-switching models capture the asymmetry better than the alternatives they try. Hong, Tu and Zhou (2007) give model-free tests for asymmetric correlations and betas.

Ang and Chen also report that their exceedance correlations differ from co-skewness as a measure of asymmetry. In the fast-switching model the co-skewness above is the first-order fingerprint in the cumulants, and the simulated exceedance correlations show the asymmetry directly. Ang and Bekaert (2002) estimate a regime with lower means, higher volatility and higher correlation, the bear regime used here.

Correlation risk is priced and hedged. Driessen, Maenhout and Vilkov (2009) find a price of correlation risk in index options against options on the components. Buraschi, Porchia and Trojani (2010) solve portfolio choice with a stochastic covariance matrix and find a separate hedging demand against correlation.

Stochastic correlation in option pricing is usually modelled with the Wishart process of Bru (1991), as in Da Fonseca, Grasselli and Tebaldi (2007) and Gourieroux and Sufana (2010). Deelstra and Simon (2017) price multi-asset options when a chain modulates the parameters, at ordinary speed. Spread options are surveyed by Carmona and Durrleman (2003).

No fast-switching or fast-factor asymptotics for correlation, in option pricing or in the joint law of returns, appear in the literature reviewed. The nearest is Fouque, Pun and Wong (2016), who treat an ambiguous, worst-case correlation between two assets whose volatilities are fast mean-reverting.

The smile for a switching volatility is known (Sircar and Papanicolaou 1999). The exchange option inherits it exactly, and the parabola above is its correlation form.

Scope

  • With fixed volatilities the correlation operator commutes with the rest, and the price is an exact mixture over the time-averaged correlation. When the volatilities also switch, the operators still have constant coefficients, and the correction is $T\sum K_{jk}\,\partial^2 C/\partial\Sigma_j\partial\Sigma_k$ over the entries of the integrated covariance matrix.
  • Under the pricing measure the drift is $r$ in every regime, so the co-skewness of the second part matters for allocation, risk and density forecasts, not for prices, unless the regime carries a risk premium.
  • A chain independent of the price shocks gives an exchange-option smile that is even in log-moneyness. A fast diffusive correlation factor whose shocks are correlated with the prices would add a term of order $\sqrt\varepsilon$, odd in moneyness, that skews the implied correlation, in the manner of the Fouque, Papanicolaou and Sircar volatility skew. That direction is not worked out here.
