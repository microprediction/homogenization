Poisson counts with a switching rate

Events whose arrival rate follows a hidden regime, and the distribution of their count.

The model

Events arrive one at a time, as insurance claims, defaults in a portfolio or requests to a server do.

Their rate depends on a hidden regime $y_t \in \{1, 2\}$: while $y_t = i$ they arrive as a Poisson process with rate $\ell_i$.

The regime switches at rate $\lambda$ in each direction. This is a Markov-modulated Poisson process.

There are two time scales: events arrive every $1/\ell_i$ on average, and the regime changes every $1/\lambda$.

Parameters: $\ell = (8, 1)$ per unit time, the process starts in the busy regime, and $N_t$ counts the events up to $t = 1$.

The quantity

The whole distribution $\Pr(N_t = k)$, $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, through the generating function

$$a_i(t) = \mathbb{E}\big[z^{N_t} \mid y_0 = i\big].$$

Averaging

When the regime switches infinitely fast, events arrive as a Poisson process with the average rate $\bar\ell = 4.5$, and the variance of the count equals its mean. Switching at a finite rate makes the count more variable than Poisson, and the corrector carries that overdispersion.

Reduction to a linear system

Condition on what happens in a short interval $dt$ at the start. While the regime is $i$, an event occurs with probability $\ell_i\,dt$ and multiplies $z^{N}$ by $z$; the regime jumps to $j$ with probability $Q_{ij}\,dt$. So

$$a_i(t + dt) = (1 - \ell_i\,dt)\,a_i(t) + \ell_i\,dt\;z\,a_i(t) + \sum_j Q_{ij}\,dt\;a_j(t) + o(dt),$$

and letting $dt \to 0$,

$$a' = \big(Q + (z-1)\operatorname{diag}\ell\big)\,a, \qquad a(0) = \mathbf 1, \qquad g_i = (z-1)\,\ell_i ,$$

a constant, complex for complex $z$. Evaluating $a(t)$ at the 64 points $z = e^{2\pi i k/64}$ on the unit circle and applying the discrete Fourier transform returns $\Pr(N_t \equiv k \bmod 64)$ for every $k$ at once.

These equal the probabilities $\Pr(N_t = k)$ up to the chance of 64 or more events. Given the regime path the count is Poisson with mean at most 8, so that chance is below $2\times10^{-35}$.

The expansion

Let the regime switch at rate $\lambda$ in each direction, and write

$$\varepsilon = \frac1\lambda, \qquad \bar g = \tfrac12(g_1 + g_2), \qquad \tilde g = \tfrac12(g_1 - g_2),$$

Here the forcing is constant:

$$\bar g = (z-1)\,\bar\ell, \qquad \tilde g = \tfrac12(z-1)(\ell_1 - \ell_2) .$$

The average $\bar g$ is what the averaged model sees. The half-difference $\tilde g$ measures how much the two regimes disagree.

First order

To first order in $\varepsilon$, at fixed $t > 0$,

$$a_i(t) = \exp\Big(\int_0^t \bar g + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_0^t \tilde g^{\,2}\Big)\Big(1 \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde g(t)\Big) + O(\varepsilon^2),$$

with the upper sign for a start in regime 1. The three factors have separate meanings, explained on the idea page.

  • The factor below is the averaged model: $$e^{\int_0^t \bar g} .$$
  • The term below is half the second cumulant of the fluctuating integral of $g$: $$\frac\varepsilon2\int_0^t\tilde g^{\,2} .$$ The square carries no conjugate, and here $g$ is complex whenever $z$ is, and real on the real axis, so the term is complex and is not a variance. For real $g$ it is half the variance, as on the idea page. It is the same for both starting regimes.
  • The bracket is the memory of the starting regime. The switch stays near its starting state for about $1/(2\lambda)$, which shifts the integral by $\pm\tilde g/(2\lambda)$.

Every order

Add and subtract the two equations for $a_1$ and $a_2$. The mean $m = \tfrac12(a_1 + a_2)$ and the normalized difference $\omega = (a_1 - a_2)/(a_1 + a_2)$ then satisfy

$$\omega' = \tilde g\,(1 - \omega^2) - 2\lambda\,\omega, \qquad \omega(0) = 0,$$ $$\log m(t) = \int_0^t \big(\bar g + \tilde g\,\omega\big), \qquad a_{1,2} = m\,(1 \pm \omega).$$

The first equation has a large restoring term $-2\lambda\omega$: the switch pulls the two regimes together at rate $2\lambda$, while $\tilde g$ pushes them apart. So $\omega$ stays close to the value that balances the two, and expanding in $\varepsilon$ finds that value order by order. Write

$$\omega = \sum_n \varepsilon^n\omega_n$$

and match powers of $\varepsilon$:

$$\omega_1 = \frac{\tilde g}{2}, \qquad \omega_{n+1} = -\frac12\Big(\omega_n' + \tilde g\sum_{i+j=n}\omega_i\,\omega_j\Big).$$

This is the two-state case of the solvability argument: at each order the restoring term can be inverted, which here is a division by $2\lambda$.

The series does not satisfy $\omega(0) = 0$.

An initial layer of width about $1/(2\lambda)$ repairs the start and is solved order by order in the same way. Its leading term adds $\mp\tfrac12\varepsilon\,\tilde g(0)\,e^{-2\lambda t}$ to the first-order bracket, which returns the bracket to $1$ at $t = 0$; when $\tilde g(0) \ne 0$ the first-order formula above is therefore accurate to $O(\varepsilon^2)$ only at fixed $t > 0$. The engine page gives the version for any number of regimes.

For the count, the shared first-order term multiplies the generating function by

$$\exp\big(\tfrac{t}{8\lambda}(z-1)^2(\ell_1 - \ell_2)^2\big),$$

which adds $t(\ell_1 - \ell_2)^2/(4\lambda)$ to the variance.

The generating function in closed form

The forcing $g_i = (z - 1)\ell_i$ is constant, so the generating function is exact:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[z^{N_T}\big]_{1,2} \;=\; &e^{(\bar g - \lambda)T}\Big(\cosh sT + \frac{\lambda \pm \tilde g}{s}\,\sinh sT\Big), \qquad s = \sqrt{\lambda^2 + \tilde g^2}, \\[4pt] \;=\; &\exp\Big(\bar g\,T + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde g^2\,T - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{4}\,\tilde g^2\big(1 - e^{-2\lambda T}\big)\Big) \Big(1 \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde g\,\big(1 - e^{-2\lambda T}\big)\Big) + O(\varepsilon^3). \end{aligned}$$

with

$$\bar g = (z - 1)\,\bar\ell, \qquad \tilde g = (z - 1)\,\tilde\ell, \qquad \bar\ell, \tilde\ell = \frac{\ell_1 \pm \ell_2}{2} .$$

Differentiating the expansion at $z = 1$ gives the mean and variance of the count. With $L = 1 - e^{-2\lambda T}$ and the upper sign for a start in regime 1,

$$\mathbb{E}[N_T] = \bar\ell\,T \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde\ell\,L,$$ $$\operatorname{Var}[N_T] = \bar\ell\,T + \varepsilon\,\tilde\ell^{\,2}\,T \pm \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\tilde\ell\,L - \varepsilon^2\,\tilde\ell^{\,2}\Big(\frac L2 + \frac{L^2}{4}\Big) .$$

These two formulas have no higher-order terms. Given the regime path the count is Poisson with mean $\Lambda = \int_0^T \ell_{y_s}\,ds$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}[N_T] = \mathbb{E}[\Lambda] + \operatorname{Var}[\Lambda],$$

and integrating the covariance of the two-state regime, which is $e^{-2\lambda|t - s|} - e^{-2\lambda(t + s)}$ in the $\pm1$ coding, gives both lines exactly.

The variance exceeds the mean by

$$\operatorname{Var}[N_T] - \mathbb{E}[N_T] = \varepsilon\,\tilde\ell^{\,2}\,T - \varepsilon^2\,\tilde\ell^{\,2}\Big(\frac L2 + \frac{L^2}{4}\Big) .$$

At fixed $T > 0$ the first term leads. It is the Green–Kubo overdispersion. The second term is the memory of the starting regime, and at horizons short against $1/\lambda$ it nearly cancels the first.

Factorial cumulants remove Poisson noise

The preceding identity extends to every order and any stochastic intensity whose transform is finite near the origin. Define the factorial cumulant generating function by

$$H_N(t)=\log\mathbb E[(1+t)^{N_T}].$$

Conditional Poisson sampling gives the exact identity

$$H_N(t)=\log\mathbb E[e^{t\Lambda_T}].$$

Therefore the $r$th factorial cumulant of $N_T$ is the ordinary $r$th cumulant of $\Lambda_T$. In particular,

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa_2^{(F)}(N_T)&=\kappa_2(N_T)-\kappa_1(N_T),\\ \kappa_3^{(F)}(N_T)&=\kappa_3(N_T)-3\kappa_2(N_T)+2\kappa_1(N_T),\\ \kappa_4^{(F)}(N_T)&=\kappa_4(N_T)-6\kappa_3(N_T)+11\kappa_2(N_T)-6\kappa_1(N_T). \end{aligned}$$

Beyond the mean, these combinations strip out Poisson shot noise and leave only fluctuations of the integrated rate. For several counts that are conditionally independent given cumulative intensities $\Lambda_1,\ldots,\Lambda_d$, the joint identity is

$$\log\mathbb E\!\left[\prod_{j=1}^d(1+t_j)^{N_j}\right] =\log\mathbb E\!\left[e^{\sum_jt_j\Lambda_j}\right].$$

Thus mixed factorial cumulants identify the joint cumulants of the integrated rates exactly. For two streams, writing $\kappa_{rs}$ for the ordinary joint cumulant with $r$ copies of $N_1$ and $s$ copies of $N_2$, the signed-Stirling conversion through bidegree $(2,2)$ is

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa^{(F)}_{11}&=\kappa_{11},& \kappa^{(F)}_{21}&=\kappa_{21}-\kappa_{11},\\ \kappa^{(F)}_{12}&=\kappa_{12}-\kappa_{11},& \kappa^{(F)}_{22}&=\kappa_{22}-\kappa_{21}-\kappa_{12}+\kappa_{11}. \end{aligned}$$

The transform is applied separately in each coordinate. Conditional independence is essential; without it, shared-event shot noise is not removed.

What common shocks add

The correction is exact. Index independent Poisson event streams by the nonempty subsets $A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}$ of coordinates that each event increments. Conditional on their cumulative intensities $\Lambda_A$, put $N_j=\sum_{A\ni j}C_A$. Then

$$\log\mathbb E\prod_{j=1}^d(1+t_j)^{N_j} =\log\mathbb E\exp\left\{\sum_{A\ne\varnothing}\Lambda_A \left(\prod_{j\in A}(1+t_j)-1\right)\right\}.$$

For two counts, write $\Lambda_0$ for the common-event intensity and $A=\Lambda_1+\Lambda_0$, $B=\Lambda_2+\Lambda_0$, $C=\Lambda_0$. Expanding the preceding identity through arbitrary bidegree $(r,s)$ gives the all-order identity

$$\boxed{\displaystyle \kappa^{(F)}_{rs}=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(r,s)} {r\choose k}{s\choose k}k!\, \kappa\!\left(A^{[r-k]},B^{[s-k]},C^{[k]}\right)}.$$

Here the brackets denote repeated arguments of the joint cumulant; when only one argument remains, it is its expectation. The coefficient counts which $k$ derivatives in each coordinate strike the bilinear common-event term. In particular, through bidegree $(2,2)$,

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa^{(F)}_{11}&=\kappa(A,B)+\mathbb EC,\\ \kappa^{(F)}_{21}&=\kappa(A,A,B)+2\kappa(A,C),\\ \kappa^{(F)}_{12}&=\kappa(A,B,B)+2\kappa(B,C),\\ \kappa^{(F)}_{22}&=\kappa(A,A,B,B)+4\kappa(A,B,C)+2\kappa(C,C). \end{aligned}$$

Thus mixed factorial cumulants identify marginal-intensity dependence only after the common-shock terms are modeled or ruled out. The sharp counterexample has deterministic $C=cT$ and no idiosyncratic intensity: $N_1=N_2\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(cT)$. The cumulative intensities have zero covariance, but $\kappa^{(F)}_{11}=cT$. At $c=0.8$ and $T=1.3$, the certificate obtains $1.04000000$.

Arbitrary integer marks

The unit-jump restriction is unnecessary. Let each common event carry an iid nonnegative integer mark $J=(J_1,J_2)$, independent of the cumulative intensities and of the other marks. Conditional on the common cumulative intensity $C$, the number of marked events is Poisson with mean $C$. If $\Lambda_1,\Lambda_2$ are the idiosyncratic cumulative intensities, then the exact factorial cumulant generating function is

$$H(t,u)=\log\mathbb E\exp\!\left\{t\Lambda_1+u\Lambda_2 +C\left(\mathbb E[(1+t)^{J_1}(1+u)^{J_2}]-1\right)\right\}.$$

Put $\mu_{ab}=\mathbb E[(J_1)_a(J_2)_b]$, where $(j)_a$ is a falling factorial, and define the integrated-rate variables

$$W_{10}=\Lambda_1+\mu_{10}C,\qquad W_{01}=\Lambda_2+\mu_{01}C,\qquad W_{ab}=\mu_{ab}C\quad(a+b\ge2).$$

Take $r$ labelled symbols of type 1 and $s$ of type 2. For a block $B$ of a set partition, let $a_B,b_B$ be its numbers of the two symbol types. The all-order marked theorem is

$$\boxed{\displaystyle \kappa^{(F)}_{rs}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{r,s}} \kappa\!\left(W_{a_Bb_B}:B\in\pi\right).}$$

This is the multivariate logarithmic Faà di Bruno formula applied to $H$. It requires the displayed falling-factorial mark moments and the corresponding integrated-rate cumulants to be finite. For example,

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa^{(F)}_{11}&=\kappa(W_{10},W_{01})+\mathbb EW_{11},\\ \kappa^{(F)}_{21}&=\kappa(W_{10},W_{10},W_{01}) +\kappa(W_{20},W_{01})+2\kappa(W_{10},W_{11})+\mathbb EW_{21}. \end{aligned}$$

When $J=(1,1)$ almost surely, every $\mu_{ab}$ vanishes except those with $a,b\le1$. Only singleton blocks and disjoint type-1/type-2 pairs survive, and the partition formula reduces exactly to the preceding ${r\choose k}{s\choose k}k!$ matching formula. Thus larger or asymmetric common marks do not invalidate factorial-cumulant identification; they change the shared-shot-noise correction through their factorial moments.

The certificate obtains the factorial-count and integrated-intensity columns independently from the regime/count master equation and a polynomial Feynman–Kac hierarchy. It checks the univariate identity through order four and the bivariate identity through bidegree $(2,2)$ for a nonreversible three-state chain. The mixed calculation agrees within $5.3\times10^{-14}$, with omitted count mass bounded by $1.6\times10^{-47}$. A separate master equation with simultaneous $(1,1)$ jumps checks the common-shock formula through every mixed order $(r,s)$ with $1\leq r,s\leq3$ within $7.9\times10^{-12}$; at $(3,3)$ the two independent values are 3.05155621 and 3.05155621. A second marked master equation uses six mark vectors, including $(3,1)$ and $(1,3)$, and agrees with the marked partition theorem through bidegree $(3,3)$ within $9.6\times10^{-10}$; the independent values of $\kappa^{(F)}_{33}$ are 1.41746379 and 1.41746379, with omitted mass below $8.7\times10^{-49}$. Substituting the degenerate mark $(1,1)$ recovers the matching formula within $1.1\times10^{-11}$. The partition calculation follows the joint-cumulant method of Leonov and Shiryaev (1959). The certificate also measures second-order decay of the univariate omitted boundary term. The conditioning argument is the defining construction of a Cox process, specialized here to the Markov-modulated Poisson process. The common-component construction is the classical bivariate Poisson model of Holgate (1964); the arbitrary-mark construction is a multivariate compound-Poisson law in the sense of Feller (1943).
