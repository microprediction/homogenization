Pulsar spin-down switching

A pulsar whose spin-down rate jumps between two values: the timing noise it produces, its Green–Kubo strength, the exact two-state formulas, and what timing data can and cannot tell apart.

The model

A pulsar rotates at frequency $\nu(t)$ and slows down. Its spin-down rate $\dot\nu$ is not constant: it switches abruptly between two values, set by the state of the magnetosphere. PSR B1931+24 spins down about 50% faster when its radio emission is on than when it is off (Kramer et al. 2006), and many pulsars switch between two spin-down rates that differ by about a percent (Lyne et al. 2010).

Model the state as a hidden Markov chain $y_t \in \{1, 2\}$ that leaves state 1 at rate $a$ and state 2 at rate $b$. The rates may differ, so the pulsar can spend most of its time in one state. The spin-down rate is $\dot\nu_{y_t}$, and frequency and phase (in cycles) are its integrals:

$$\nu(t) = \nu(0) + \int_0^t \dot\nu_{y_s}\,ds, \qquad \phi(t) = \phi(0) + \int_0^t \nu(s)\,ds .$$

The chain starts from its stationary law, since an observer does not know the state when timing begins.

There are two time scales. The state persists for $1/a$ or $1/b$, from days to years.

The pulsar is timed over a span $T$ of years. The expansion parameter is $1/(\gamma T)$ with $\gamma = a + b$.

Parameters (PSR B1931+24, from Kramer et al. 2006): $\nu = 1.2289688$ Hz, $\dot\nu_{\mathrm{on}} = -16.3\times10^{-15}$ Hz/s, $\dot\nu_{\mathrm{off}} = -10.8\times10^{-15}$ Hz/s, on phases of 5 to 10 days, off phases of 25 to 35 days; mean durations $1/a = 7.5$ days and $1/b = 30$ days.

The quantity

Write $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2)$ for the stationary law and $\bar{\dot\nu} = \pi_1\dot\nu_1 + \pi_2\dot\nu_2$ for the averaged spin-down rate. Removing it leaves the fluctuation of the rate,

$$g(y) = \dot\nu_y - \bar{\dot\nu}, \qquad g_1 = \pi_2\Delta, \qquad g_2 = -\pi_1\Delta, \qquad \Delta = \dot\nu_1 - \dot\nu_2 .$$

The frequency residual and the phase residual after the averaged spin-down are

$$X(T) = \int_0^T g(y_s)\,ds, \qquad \Phi(T) = \int_0^T X(t)\,dt = \int_0^T (T - s)\,g(y_s)\,ds .$$

The timing residual in seconds is $\Phi/\nu$. We want the mean, the variance and the full distribution of $X$ and $\Phi$, the power spectrum of the timing residual, and the residual left after the usual timing fit.

Averaging

When the state switches infinitely fast, the pulsar spins down at the constant rate $\bar{\dot\nu}$. Both residuals have mean zero, and the averaged model predicts no timing noise.

Reduction to a linear system

Let $Q$ be the generator of the chain and $G = \operatorname{diag}(g_1, g_2)$. Condition on the state over the first instant. The characteristic function of $X$ started in state $i$, $a_i(T) = \mathbb{E}_i\,e^{iuX(T)}$, then solves

$$a' = \big(Q + iu\,G\big)\,a, \qquad a(0) = \mathbf 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}\,e^{iuX(T)} = \pi\cdot a(T).$$

This is the engine's linear system with an imaginary, constant forcing. The phase weights the rate at time $s$ by $T - s$, and the same argument gives a forcing that grows with time:

$$a' = \big(Q + iu\,t\,G\big)\,a, \qquad a(0) = \mathbf 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}\,e^{iu\Phi(T)} = \pi\cdot a(T).$$

The first instant carries the largest weight, $T$, which is why the forcing at time $t$ is $iu\,t\,G$.

The first-order rule

The variance of an integral is the double integral of the covariance. For a stationary rate with covariance $C(\tau) = \operatorname{Cov}\big(\dot\nu_{y_0}, \dot\nu_{y_\tau}\big)$,

$$\operatorname{Var} X(T) = 2\int_0^T (T - \tau)\,C(\tau)\,d\tau .$$

When $T$ is long against the correlation time, the factor $T - \tau$ is close to $T$ wherever $C$ is not negligible. Each spell in a state adds a nearly independent kick to the frequency, so the kicks add like a random walk:

$$\operatorname{Var} X(T) \approx 2KT, \qquad K = \int_0^\infty C(\tau)\,d\tau = -\pi\cdot\big(g\;Q^{\#}g\big).$$

$K$ is the Green–Kubo integral of the spin-down rate, and $Q^{\#}$ is the group inverse of the generator, as on the general page. The neglected part, $-2\int_0^\infty \tau\,C(\tau)\,d\tau$, is a constant: the initial layer.

The phase integrates the frequency again. Its variance is

$$\operatorname{Var} \Phi(T) = 2\int_0^T C(\tau)\,\Big(\frac{(T - \tau)^3}{3} + \frac{\tau\,(T - \tau)^2}{2}\Big)\,d\tau \approx \frac23\,K\,T^3 .$$

To first order the frequency residual is a random walk and the phase residual is its integral. Both are Gaussian, with the characteristic functions

$$\mathbb{E}\,e^{iuX(T)} \approx e^{-u^2 KT}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\,e^{iu\Phi(T)} \approx e^{-u^2 KT^3/3} .$$

The rates, the switching times and the asymmetry of the chain enter only through $\bar{\dot\nu}$ and $K$.

The two-state results written out

For two states the covariance is a single exponential, $C(\tau) = \pi_1\pi_2\Delta^2 e^{-\gamma\tau}$, and every integral above can be done. The variances are exact for all $T$:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var} X(T) &= 2K\Big(T - \frac{1 - e^{-\gamma T}}{\gamma}\Big), \\ \operatorname{Var} \Phi(T) &= 2K\Big(\frac{T^3}{3} - \frac{T^2}{2\gamma} + \frac{1 - e^{-\gamma T}}{\gamma^3} - \frac{T\,e^{-\gamma T}}{\gamma^2}\Big), \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} K &= \frac{\pi_1\pi_2\,\Delta^2}{\gamma}, \\ \gamma &= a + b, \\ \pi_1 &= b/\gamma, \qquad \pi_2 = a/\gamma, \\ \Delta &= \dot\nu_1 - \dot\nu_2 . \end{aligned}$$

The layer constant of the frequency variance is $-2K/\gamma$. In the phase variance the first correction to the Green–Kubo form is the $T^2$ term, $-KT^2/\gamma$, and the constant is $2K/\gamma^3$.

The characteristic function of the frequency residual follows from the $2\times2$ matrix exponential. Since $\pi\cdot g = 0$, the term in $\pi\cdot(Q + sG)\mathbf 1$ drops out, and with $s = iu$

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\,e^{sX(T)} &= e^{(s\Sigma - \gamma)T/2}\,\Big(\cosh DT + \frac{\gamma - s\Sigma}{2D}\,\sinh DT\Big), \\ \lambda(s) &= \tfrac12\,(s\Sigma - \gamma) + D, \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} D &= \tfrac12\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 2\gamma\,s\,\Sigma + s^2\Delta^2}, \\ \Sigma &= (\pi_2 - \pi_1)\,\Delta . \end{aligned}$$

The expression is even in $D$, so the branch of the square root does not matter. For large $T$ the logarithm of the characteristic function is $\lambda(s)\,T$ plus a constant, where $\lambda(s)$ is the largest eigenvalue of $Q + sG$. Its Taylor coefficients are the cumulant rates:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda(s) &= K s^2 + \lambda_3 s^3 + \lambda_4 s^4 + \cdots, \\ \kappa_3\big(X(T)\big) &\approx 6\,\lambda_3\,T, \qquad \kappa_3\big(\Phi(T)\big) \approx \tfrac32\,\lambda_3\,T^4, \\ \operatorname{skew} X(T) &\approx \frac{3\,(\pi_2 - \pi_1)\operatorname{sgn}\Delta}{\sqrt{2\pi_1\pi_2}\,\sqrt{\gamma T}}, \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \lambda_3 &= \frac{\pi_1\pi_2\,(\pi_2 - \pi_1)\,\Delta^3}{\gamma^2}, \\ \lambda_4 &= \frac{\pi_1\pi_2\,(1 - 5\pi_1\pi_2)\,\Delta^4}{\gamma^3} . \end{aligned}$$

The skewness is a second-order effect: it falls like $(\gamma T)^{-1/2}$ relative to the Gaussian. It vanishes when the two states are equally occupied. With unequal occupation the rare state produces bursts of extra frequency change, and the residual leans towards them.

The phase weights the rate by $T - s$, which varies slowly on the switching time. To leading order each stretch contributes its own growth rate:

$$\log\mathbb{E}\,e^{s\Phi(T)} \approx \int_0^T \lambda(s\,t)\,dt .$$

This gives the phase variance $\tfrac23KT^3$ and the third cumulant above.

The power spectrum

The spin-down rate is a random telegraph signal. Its spectrum is a Lorentzian, and the phase spectrum divides it by $(2\pi f)^4$ because phase is the double integral of the rate:

$$\begin{aligned} S_{\dot\nu}(f) &= \frac{2\pi_1\pi_2\,\Delta^2\,\gamma}{\gamma^2 + (2\pi f)^2}, \\ S_{\phi}(f) &= \frac{S_{\dot\nu}(f)}{(2\pi f)^4}, \qquad S_{r}(f) = \frac{S_{\phi}(f)}{\nu^2} . \end{aligned}$$

The spectra are two-sided, per unit frequency, and $S_r$ is the spectrum of the timing residual in seconds. Below the corner frequency $\gamma/2\pi$ the rate spectrum is flat at $S_{\dot\nu}(0) = 2K$, and the timing residual has the $f^{-4}$ spectrum of a random walk in frequency. Above the corner it steepens to $f^{-6}$.

After a timing fit

Timing analyses fit phase, frequency and spin-down to the arrival times, so a quadratic in $t$ is removed and $\bar{\dot\nu}$ need not be known. At first order $\Phi$ is an integrated random walk with covariance

$$\operatorname{Cov}\big(\Phi(t), \Phi(t')\big) = 2K\int_0^{\min(t, t')} (t - r)(t' - r)\,dr .$$

Project it onto quadratics on $[0, T]$ and average the square of what is left over the span:

$$\frac1T\,\mathbb{E}\int_0^T r(t)^2\,dt = 2K\,T^3\Big(\frac1{12} - \frac{209}{2520}\Big) = \frac{2K\,T^3}{2520} .$$

Here $r$ is the phase residual after the fit. Before the fit the same average is $KT^3/6$, so the fit removes all but $1/210$ of the mean-square phase wander. The root-mean-square timing residual after the fit, in seconds, is

$$r_{\mathrm{rms}} = \frac1\nu\sqrt{\frac{2K\,T^3}{2520}} .$$

What timing data identify

The two-state chain has four parameters: $\dot\nu_1$, $\dot\nu_2$, $a$ and $b$. To first order timing data see two numbers, the averaged spin-down $\bar{\dot\nu}$ and the Green–Kubo integral $K$. Any two chains that share them give the same variance, the same Gaussian law and the same low-frequency spectrum.

For example, keep the switching time of B1931+24 and make the two states equally likely, with the rates moved so that the average and $K$ are unchanged. Both chains have $1/\gamma = 6$ days, $\bar{\dot\nu} = -11.90\times10^{-15}$ Hz/s and $K = 2.51\times10^{-24}$ Hz$^2$/s. The frequency and timing residuals are identical in variance, and only the skewness differs:

The third cumulant gives $\Sigma = (\pi_2 - \pi_1)\Delta$. Then $\Delta^2 = \Sigma^2 + 4K\gamma$ closes the system. These corrections are smaller by $(\gamma T)^{-1/2}$ and $(\gamma T)^{-1}$, so over spans of many switching times they are hard to measure.

Quasi-periodic switching

Real switching is often close to periodic. The on and off phases of B1931+24 last 5 to 10 and 25 to 35 days, far less variable than exponential durations with the same means.

The frequency then wanders much less, and $K$ must come from an alternating renewal process. With durations of mean $m_1$, $m_2$ and standard deviations $s_1$, $s_2$, the occupation-time central limit theorem gives

$$K = \frac{\Delta^2\,\big(m_2^2\,s_1^2 + m_1^2\,s_2^2\big)}{2\,(m_1 + m_2)^3} .$$

Exponential durations, $s_i = m_i$, give back $\pi_1\pi_2\Delta^2/\gamma$. Strictly periodic switching, $s_1 = s_2 = 0$, gives $K = 0$: the frequency residual stays bounded and there is no random walk.

The same answer comes from the Markov machinery. Replace each phase by a chain of $k$ exponential stages, an Erlang duration with standard deviation $m/\sqrt k$. The Green–Kubo formula $-\pi\cdot(g\,Q^{\#}g)$ on the resulting cyclic chain reproduces the renewal formula exactly.

For B1931+24 with uniform durations on 5 to 10 and 25 to 35 days, $K = 5.81\times10^{-26}$ Hz$^2$/s, about 43 times smaller than the Markov value. Timing noise scales as $\sqrt K$, so the quasi-periodic residuals are about 6.6 times smaller than the Markov ones.

Prior art

That timing noise comes from switching between spin-down rates is the finding of Kramer et al. (2006) for B1931+24 and of Lyne et al. (2010) for many pulsars. Lyne et al. simulated it directly: two rates with dithered switching times, integrated twice, sampled, with a quadratic removed. Nothing here claims that observation.

The random walk in frequency, with its $f^{-4}$ residual spectrum, is one of the classical timing-noise models of Boynton et al. (1972) and Cordes and Helfand (1980). Hobbs, Lyne and Kramer (2010) analyse timing irregularities across hundreds of pulsars, and van Haasteren and Levin (2012) treat timing noise as a Gaussian process with a red spectrum. The telegraph covariance, its Lorentzian spectrum and the central limit theorem for occupation times of alternating renewal processes are classical.

The fast-switching reading adds the following to these facts. The strength of the random walk is a Green–Kubo integral, as in Green (1954) and Kubo (1957). The two-state variances, characteristic function and cumulant rates are written out for finite spans, with the layer constant.

The post-fit residual has a closed first-order form. To first order the timing data identify $\bar{\dot\nu}$ and $K$, not the individual rates and switching times, and quasi-periodic switching enters only through a smaller $K$.
