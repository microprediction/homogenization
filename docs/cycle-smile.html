The direction of a regime cycle in the Heston smile

A chain that cycles through regimes in one direction moves the smile, although its averages and covariances do not depend on the direction.

The smile from fast volatility

When volatility fluctuates fast and independently of the stock, the option price is Black–Scholes averaged over integrated variance (Hull and White 1987). Expanding that average around the mean variance gives a smile at first order in the fluctuation time scale.

Sircar and Papanicolaou (1999), section 3.3, derive it for a stationary ergodic volatility driver whose integrated fluctuations obey a central limit theorem, which a finite-state Markov chain does. The implied volatility is quadratic in log-moneyness, with a curvature set by the variance of integrated variance. Fouque, Lorig and Sircar (2016) carry the fast-factor expansion to second order with correlation and a terminal layer.

In that work the fast driver is a one-dimensional diffusion or a generic ergodic process whose correlation function enters through a variance. A one-dimensional diffusion is reversible, so its Green–Kubo matrix is symmetric. The antisymmetric part, which measures the direction of a cycle, does not appear.

The central limit condition sets the order of the correction. A stationary ergodic driver with long memory, whose correlation decays like $t^{-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$, has no finite Green–Kubo integral. With $\varepsilon$ the fluctuation time scale, the variance of its integrated variance scales like $\varepsilon^\alpha$ in place of $\varepsilon$, and so does the smile correction. The chain below mixes exponentially fast and has the order-$\varepsilon$ correction.

The model

The log price $X_t$ and the variance $v_t$ follow Heston's model with zero correlation. The variance level and the vol-of-vol depend on a hidden regime $y_t$:

$$dX_t = -\tfrac12 v_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t, \qquad dv_t = \kappa\,\big(\theta_{y_t} - v_t\big)\,dt + \xi_{y_t}\sqrt{v_t}\,dZ_t .$$

The regime is a three-state chain that cycles mostly one way, $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$. The reverse chain has the same stationary law and the same symmetric Green–Kubo matrix. The variance reverts over $1/\kappa$, half a year, while the regimes last a few weeks.

Parameters: $\kappa = 2$; levels $\theta = (0.02,\ 0.10,\ 0.05)$; vol-of-vol $\xi = (0.2,\ 0.4,\ 1.0)$; $v_0 = 0.04$; $S_0 = 100$; zero interest; one year to expiry. Forward rate 2 and reverse rate 0.1 per year along the cycle, multiplied by a speed factor $m$.

The quantity

European call prices and their Black–Scholes implied volatilities, for a regime drawn from its stationary law. The prices come from the characteristic function of the return,

$$\phi(u) = \mathbb{E}\big[e^{iu(X_T - X_0)} \mid v_0\big],$$

through Lewis's formula:

$$C = S_0 - \frac{\sqrt{S_0K}}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \operatorname{Re}\big[e^{iu\log(S_0/K)}\,\phi(u - \tfrac i2)\big]\,\frac{du}{u^2 + 1/4}.$$

Averaging

When the chain switches infinitely fast, the limit is Heston's model with the stationary averages $\bar\theta$ and $\bar\xi^2$.

The first-order rule

After the Fourier transform in $x$, the switched parameters $\theta$ and $\xi^2$ multiply two operators in the variance:

$$A_\theta = \kappa\,\partial_v, \qquad A_{\xi^2} = \tfrac12\,v\,\partial_{vv} .$$

They do not commute:

$$\big[A_\theta,\,A_{\xi^2}\big] f = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\big(\partial_v(v f'') - v f'''\big) = \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\,f'' .$$

By the first-order rule, the averaged operator gains

$$\sum_{j,k} K^{\mathrm{sym}}_{jk}\,A_j A_k \;+\; K^{\mathrm{anti}}_{\theta\xi^2}\,\tfrac{\kappa}{2}\,\partial_{vv}, \qquad K^{\mathrm{anti}}_{\theta\xi^2} = \tfrac12\big(K_{\theta\xi^2} - K_{\xi^2\theta}\big).$$

The last term adds a constant diffusion to the variance. Its sign flips when the cycle is reversed, and the symmetric terms do not change.

The intuition is a lag. The variance follows its level with a delay of about $1/\kappa$.

When high vol-of-vol tends to follow a high level, it arrives while the variance is high and shakes it harder. When high vol-of-vol comes first, it acts on a low variance.

Method

For each Fourier frequency the coupled equations for the three regimes are solved on one finite-difference grid in $v$, which gives the numerical solution. The rule is applied on the same grid, as a first-order Duhamel correction to the averaged operator, so both share the discretisation error.

The full rule's error falls by about four for each doubling, as a second-order remainder should. The effect of the direction falls by about two, as a first-order effect should, and at the fastest speed the rule reproduces it within about 20% at every strike.

Keeping only the symmetric part is worse than no correction. In the forward direction the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of the correction largely cancel, so either one alone misses by more than the average does.
