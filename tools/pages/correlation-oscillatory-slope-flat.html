Oscillatory-slope covariance

A bounded adiabatic slope produces a moving Gaussian constant, not necessarily a limit.

Finite logarithmic slope need not converge. If it changes negligibly on bounded windows, the normalized covariance tracks the Gaussian constant at the current slope; an oscillating slope can therefore make that covariance alternate forever between distinct subsequential limits.

The moving-constant theorem

Use the fixed-maturity finite-chain and tangent-gauge setup of the constant-slope theorem. Put $n=mT$, $L_n=\tfrac12\log n$, $d_n=h'(L_n)$, and let $G\sim N(0,\Gamma)$ on $\operatorname{Ran}\Gamma$.

Suppose $h$ is eventually $C^2$ and increasing, and for some fixed constants

$$0<c_-\le h'(x)\le c_+<\infty,\qquad h''(x)\longrightarrow0.$$

Then for $p=1,2$, uniformly over arbitrary initial regime priors,

$$e^{p h(L_n)}\mathbb E\Psi_{h,r}(W_{m,T})^p -\mathbb E\rho(G)^{p d_n}\longrightarrow0.$$

Consequently, writing $V_{c,\rho,\Gamma}=\operatorname{Var}\{\rho(G)^c\}$,

$$e^{2h(L_n)}\operatorname{Var}(\Psi_{h,r}(W_{m,T})) -V_{d_n,\rho,\Gamma}\longrightarrow0.$$

If $d_n\to c$, this reduces to the constant in the critical-slope theorem. Without convergence of $d_n$, replacing the moving constant by one fixed number is generally false.

Proof and error scope

For fixed $z$ away from zero, tangent homogeneity and Taylor's theorem give

$$h(L_n)-h(L_n-\log\rho(z)+o(1))=d_n\log\rho(z)+o(1).$$

More precisely, on $|\log\rho(z)|\le R$, the profile remainder is bounded by $\tfrac12R^2\sup_{|u-L_n|\le R+o(1)}|h''(u)|+o(1)$. This tends to zero uniformly for each fixed $R$.

The lower slope bound controls the origin, while the upper bound gives a common polynomial envelope. The finite-chain spectral tail bound supplies uniform integrability, so the uniform central limit theorem passes the first two moments to the varying functions $\rho(G)^{p d_n}$.

The displayed covariance approximation has additive error $o(1)$ after multiplication by $e^{2h(L_n)}$. Under only $h''\to0$, no universal rate is claimed: a rate also requires quantitative curvature, tangent-gauge and finite-chain tail moduli.

An explicit nonconvergent example

Let $u=\sqrt{1+t}$ and

$$h(t)=t+\frac{1-u\cos(3(u-1))}{3}+\frac{\sin(3(u-1))}{9}.$$

Then $h(0)=0$ and

$$h'(t)=1+\frac12\sin(3(\sqrt{1+t}-1)),\qquad h''(t)=\frac{3\cos(3(\sqrt{1+t}-1))}{4\sqrt{1+t}}.$$

Thus $h'$ stays in $[1/2,3/2]$, has no limit, and satisfies the theorem. Along its alternating minimum and maximum phases, the scalar Gaussian constants are

$$V_{1/2,|\cdot|,1}=0.121906320736,\qquad V_{3/2,|\cdot|,1}=0.856100341809,$$

so the normalized variance has at least two distinct subsequential limits.

Literature and scope

Ney and Nummelin (1987, Part I) provide the spectral Markov-additive limit theory. Kovchegov, Meredith and Nir (2010) derive the Bessel occupation densities used by the certificate; the moving-constant result is derived here.

This is a fixed-feature, fixed-positive-maturity scalar variance theorem. Singular $\Gamma$ is handled on its support, and no covariance-rank statement after integrating distinct maturity loadings is made.

If the slope tends to zero, the leading Gaussian functional becomes deterministic and a different normalization is needed. The vanishing-slope theorem identifies that phase.

The condition $h''\to0$ is essential for replacing a local profile by one slope. The translation-hull theorem treats arbitrary bounded positive slopes and gives an equal-slope periodic counterexample.
