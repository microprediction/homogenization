The likelihood of a switching short rate

A short rate observed at discrete times, with a level and volatility switched by a hidden regime much faster than the sampling: the first-order transition density, the composite likelihood it gives, what it identifies, and the one-sided test for switching it implies.

The model

The short rate follows a Vasicek model whose mean level and variance depend on a hidden Markov chain $y_t$:

$$dx_t = \kappa\,\big(\theta_{y_t} - x_t\big)\,dt + \sigma_{y_t}\,dW_t .$$

The rate is observed every $\Delta$, giving $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n$. The chain is not observed. It switches many times between observations, so $\lambda\Delta \gg 1$ for switching rates $\lambda$.

There are three time scales: the regimes last about $1/\lambda$, observations come every $\Delta$, and the rate reverts over $1/\kappa$.

Parameters for the checks: $\kappa = 0.5$, $\theta = (0.08,\ 0.02)$, $\sigma^2 = (0.0009,\ 0.0001)$, monthly observations $\Delta = 1/12$.

The quantity

The transition density $p_\pi(x_{k+1} \mid x_k)$ of the next observation, with the regime drawn from its stationary law $\pi$ at the start of the interval and summed at the end. Summed over the observations it gives

$$\ell_C = \sum_{k} \log p_\pi\big(x_{k+1} \mid x_k\big).$$

This is a composite likelihood in the sense of Lindsay (1988): a sum of log densities of pairs, each exact on its own, not the likelihood of the whole sample. The observed rate is not Markov by itself. Past observations carry information about the current regime, and the exact likelihood $\ell_F$ propagates that filter, as in Hamilton (1989).

Fast switching makes the two close, but not for the obvious reason. The regime at the end of an interval is independent of the regime at its start up to $e^{-2\lambda\Delta}$, yet the observations inside the interval move the filter away from $\pi$ by an amount of order $1/\lambda$, which is not small at the order kept here.

What closes the gap is that the leading kernel forgets the starting regime. Let $K_{ij}(x, x')$ be the density of $x'$ together with the ending regime $j$, given $x$ and the starting regime $i$. To first order

$$K = p_0\,\mathbf 1\,\pi^{\top} + \frac1\lambda\,K_1 + O(\lambda^{-2}),$$

with $p_0$ the averaged Gaussian density below. A filter $\nu = \pi + r/\lambda + O(\lambda^{-2})$ has $r\cdot\mathbf 1 = 0$, so its predictive density $\nu K\mathbf 1$ differs from the stationary-start density $\pi K\mathbf 1$ by $r\cdot(p_0\mathbf 1)/\lambda + O(\lambda^{-2}) = O(\lambda^{-2})$. The updated filter is again $\pi + O(1/\lambda)$, and on a panel of $N$ transitions started from $\pi$,

$$\ell_F - \ell_C = O\big(N/\lambda^2\big).$$

The first-order formulas below are therefore also the first-order terms of the exact likelihood. A start in a known regime changes the first transition density at order $1/\lambda$; after that observation the filter is $\pi + O(1/\lambda)$ and the remaining gap is again $O(N/\lambda^2)$.

The bound is for a fixed panel. It says nothing about $N$ growing with $\lambda$, and the optimality results quoted below for full-likelihood tests are not claimed for $\ell_C$.

Averaging

When the chain switches infinitely fast the rate is Vasicek with averaged parameters, observed as an AR(1):

$$x_{k+1} = \bar\theta + (x_k - \bar\theta)\,e^{-\kappa\Delta} + \sqrt v\,Z_k, \qquad v = \bar s\,\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa\Delta}}{2\kappa}, \qquad Z_k \sim N(0, 1),$$

with $\bar\theta = \pi\cdot\theta$ and $\bar s = \pi\cdot\sigma^2$. The transition density is the Gaussian $f(x_{k+1})$ with mean $m_k = \bar\theta + (x_k - \bar\theta)e^{-\kappa\Delta}$ and variance $v$.

Reduction to a linear system

The Laplace transform of the next observation is exponential-affine. Try $\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-c\,x_{k+1}} \mid x_k,\ y\big] = e^{-B(\Delta)\,x_k}\,a_y(\Delta)$. With no discounting the coefficient solves $B' = -\kappa B$ from $B(0) = c$, so

$$B(t) = c\,e^{-\kappa t},$$

the same in every regime. What remains is

$$a' = \big(Q + \operatorname{diag} g\big)\,a, \qquad a(0) = \mathbf 1, \qquad g_i(t) = -\kappa\,\theta_i\,B(t) + \tfrac12\,\sigma_i^2\,B(t)^2 .$$

The first-order transition density

Let $u = \theta - \bar\theta$ and $w = \sigma^2 - \bar s$ be the fluctuations of level and variance across regimes. The first-order correction to $\log(\pi\cdot a)$ is the Green–Kubo integral of the fluctuating forcing, as on the yield-curve page:

$$\int_0^\Delta \mathcal K\big(\tilde g, \tilde g\big)\,dt, \qquad \tilde g = -\kappa\,u\,B + \tfrac12\,w\,B^2, \qquad \mathcal K(f, h) = -\pi\cdot\big(f\,Q^{\#}h\big).$$

Since $B = c\,e^{-\kappa t}$, this is a polynomial in $c$:

$$\int_0^\Delta \mathcal K(\tilde g, \tilde g)\,dt = A_2\,c^2 + A_3\,c^3 + A_4\,c^4,$$ $$\begin{aligned} A_2 &= \kappa^2\,\mathcal K(u, u)\,\frac{1 - e^{-2\kappa\Delta}}{2\kappa}, \\ A_3 &= -\kappa\,\mathcal K_{\mathrm{sym}}(u, w)\,\frac{1 - e^{-3\kappa\Delta}}{3\kappa}, \\ A_4 &= \tfrac14\,\mathcal K(w, w)\,\frac{1 - e^{-4\kappa\Delta}}{4\kappa}. \end{aligned}$$

A factor $c^n$ on the transform is the $n$-th derivative of the Gaussian density, and that derivative is a Hermite polynomial times the density:

$$f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\,v^{-n/2}\,\mathrm{He}_n(z)\,f(x), \qquad z = \frac{x - m_k}{\sqrt v}.$$

So the transition density and the composite likelihood are, to first order,

$$\begin{aligned} p(x_{k+1} \mid x_k) &= f(x_{k+1})\Big(1 + \frac{A_2}{v}\,\mathrm{He}_2(z_k) - \frac{A_3}{v^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{He}_3(z_k) + \frac{A_4}{v^2}\,\mathrm{He}_4(z_k)\Big), \\ \ell_C &= \sum_k \Big(\log f(x_{k+1}) + \frac{A_2}{v}\,\mathrm{He}_2(z_k) - \frac{A_3}{v^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{He}_3(z_k) + \frac{A_4}{v^2}\,\mathrm{He}_4(z_k)\Big), \end{aligned}$$ $$\mathrm{He}_2(z) = z^2 - 1, \qquad \mathrm{He}_3(z) = z^3 - 3z, \qquad \mathrm{He}_4(z) = z^4 - 6z^2 + 3 .$$

The terms have plain meanings. $A_2$ widens the density, as extra variance would.

$A_3$ skews it, through the covariance between a regime's level and its volatility. $A_4$ fattens its tails, through the variance of the volatility.

What the composite likelihood identifies

  • To first order the chain enters only through the $2\times2$ Green–Kubo matrix of $(u, w)$, the same matrix that the yield curve sees. Different chains with the same averages and matrix have the same likelihood to first order.
  • The $\mathrm{He}_2$ term is indistinguishable from a larger variance: the data see $v + 2A_2$, which is proportional to $\bar s + 2\kappa^2\mathcal K(u, u)$. The yield curve sees the same combination, so neither the time series nor the cross section separates the average variance from the level's Green–Kubo term.
  • $A_3$ and $A_4$ are identified, and they give $\mathcal K_{\mathrm{sym}}(u, w)$ and $\mathcal K(w, w)$.
  • The matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A_3^2 \le 4A_2A_4$ by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $e^{-2\kappa t}$ and $e^{-4\kappa t}$.

The test for switching

With no switching the matrix is zero. The scores for $A_3$ and $A_4$ at zero are sums of Hermite polynomials of the AR(1) residuals, whose sample means are the residual skewness and excess kurtosis:

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial A_3} = -\frac{1}{v^{3/2}}\sum_k \mathrm{He}_3(z_k), \qquad \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial A_4} = \frac{1}{v^2}\sum_k \mathrm{He}_4(z_k).$$

Since $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_3^2] = 6$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{He}_4^2] = 24$ under the null, the standardized scores are $Z_3 = \sqrt n\,\hat S/\sqrt 6$ and $Z_4 = \sqrt n\,\hat K/\sqrt{24}$. Their sum of squares is the Jarque–Bera statistic $n(\hat S^2/6 + \hat K^2/24)$ of the AR(1) residuals, which is two-sided in kurtosis: it squares a negative $\hat K$ as readily as a positive one.

The model does not allow that direction. $A_4 \ge 0$, and the constraint $A_3^2 \le 4A_2A_4$ does not bind once $A_2$ is absorbed into the Gaussian variance, since any $A_3$ and any $A_4 > 0$ satisfy it for $A_2$ large enough. The local alternatives fill the half-plane $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R_+$ in $(A_3, A_4)$, and the score is projected onto it (Self and Liang 1987; Shapiro 1988):

$$\mathrm{LM}_+ = Z_3^2 + \max(Z_4, 0)^2 = n\Big(\frac{\hat S^2}{6} + \frac{\max(\hat K, 0)^2}{24}\Big).$$

Under the Gaussian null the two scores are asymptotically independent standard normals, so $\mathrm{LM}_+$ has the chi-bar-square law $\tfrac12\chi_1^2 + \tfrac12\chi_2^2$. Its 95% point is 5.138, against 5.991 for the $\chi_2^2$ law of the two-sided statistic. Negative sample kurtosis contributes nothing: it is evidence against the model, not evidence of a small switching effect.

The Jarque–Bera statistic remains the omnibus test of normality. $\mathrm{LM}_+$ is the score test of this model, in which the only route to non-normality is a positive variance of the volatility.

This is an information-matrix-type test, of the family that Carrasco, Hu and Ploberger (2014) show to be optimal for Markov switching when built from the full likelihood, building on White (1982) and Chesher (1984). Built from the composite likelihood, it carries no such optimality claim. What the continuous-time model adds is the structure of the coefficients: they are Green–Kubo integrals with explicit time factors, bound by the inequality above.

Tests for switching are usually non-standard because the switching parameters are not identified under the null (Hansen 1992; Garcia 1998; Cho and White 2007). In the fast-switching expansion the parameters are the Green–Kubo entries, which are identified. The null is the zero matrix, a boundary point of the positive semidefinite cone, which is why the score is projected.

Moment diagnostics follow from the same expansion:

$$\hat A_3 = -\frac{\hat S\,v^{3/2}}{6}, \qquad \hat A_4 = \frac{\hat K\,v^2}{24} .$$

They are unconstrained. A negative $\hat A_4$ is not a feasible value under the model, and a constrained fit should impose the positive semidefinite matrix as a whole rather than clip the two separately.

Scope

The expansion needs many regime changes between observations. When regimes last longer than the sampling interval, the regimes at successive observations are correlated, the composite likelihood is far from the exact one, and the exact one needs the filter of Hamilton (1989).

A switching variance alone gives the classic normal variance mixture of Clark (1973). For closed-form likelihood expansions of diffusions without regimes see Aït-Sahalia (2002).
