What sets the homogenization scale?

Ergodicity gives averaging. The covariance tail decides the size of the first fluctuation correction.

A fast finite-state chain has an integrable covariance, so its symmetric correction is proportional to its correlation time. That conclusion is not a consequence of stationarity and ergodicity alone. Long memory changes the power of the small parameter. Conversely, a vanishing Green–Kubo integral can make averaging faster than the usual diffusive rate, while a periodic ergodic driver can have no convergent Green–Kubo integral at all.

The exact starting point

Let $X_t$ be a centered stationary scalar process with covariance $C(t)=\mathbb E[X_0X_t]$, and accelerate it to $X_{t/\varepsilon}$. For

$$A_\varepsilon(T)=\int_0^T X_{t/\varepsilon}\,dt,$$

stationarity alone gives the exact identity

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) =2\int_0^T(T-u)C(u/\varepsilon)\,du =2\varepsilon\int_0^{T/\varepsilon}(T-\varepsilon s)C(s)\,ds.$$

Everything below follows from this formula or its equivalent spectral form. Ergodicity is relevant to the averaged limit, but it does not determine the rate at which the variance vanishes.

The low-frequency theorem

The covariance-tail results below require eventual sign and regular variation in the time domain. A spectral formulation removes that restriction. Write Bochner's one-sided representation as

$$C(t)=\int_{[0,\infty)}\cos(\omega t)\,\mu(d\omega).$$

Fubini's theorem gives the exact Fejér-kernel identity

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) =T^2\mu(\{0\})+2\varepsilon^2\int_{(0,\infty)} \frac{1-\cos(\omega T/\varepsilon)}{\omega^2}\,\mu(d\omega).$$

The atom is not optional under stationarity alone. The continuous-time mean ergodic theorem identifies

$$\mu(\{0\})=\left\|P_{\rm inv}X_0\right\|_2^2,$$

where $P_{\rm inv}$ is projection onto the shift-invariant subspace. Indeed, with $R=T/\varepsilon$,

$$\frac{A_\varepsilon(T)}T=\frac1R\int_0^R X_s\,ds \longrightarrow P_{\rm inv}X_0\quad\hbox{in }L^2.$$

Thus a centered ergodic process has $\mu(\{0\})=0$. More generally, zero mass at frequency zero is exactly the condition needed for this particular observable to average in mean square; it does not by itself imply that the whole stationary system is ergodic. If $\mu(\{0\})>0$, the variance converges to $T^2\mu(\{0\})$ and no fluctuation rescaling can repair the failed averaging limit. This is the spectral projection underlying von Neumann's mean ergodic theorem (1932).

Assume now that $\mu(\{0\})=0$, put $F(x)=\mu((0,x])$, and suppose that the cumulative spectral mass is regularly varying:

$$F(x)\sim b\,x^\alpha L(1/x), \qquad b>0,\quad0<\alpha<2,$$

where $L$ is slowly varying. No density is required. Then, for each fixed $T>0$,

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) \sim\frac{\alpha b\pi}{\Gamma(3-\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)} T^{2-\alpha}\varepsilon^\alpha L(T/\varepsilon).$$

To prove it, scale the Stieltjes measures by $\mu_\lambda(B)=\mu(\lambda B)/F(\lambda)$, where $\lambda=\varepsilon/T$. Regular variation gives vague convergence to $\alpha x^{\alpha-1}dx$. The kernel $2(1-\cos x)/x^2$ is bounded at zero; Potter bounds and $\alpha<2$ control its tail. Frequencies bounded away from zero contribute only $O(\varepsilon^2)$, and the limit uses

$$\int_0^\infty(1-\cos x)x^{\alpha-3}dx =\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma(3-\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)}.$$

If the measure has a density $f(\omega)\sim a\omega^{\alpha-1}L(1/\omega)$, then $b=a/\alpha$ and the coefficient reduces to $a\pi/[\Gamma(3-\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)]$, the density formula. The cumulative-mass theorem also covers singular and pure-point spectra.

This single formula includes long memory for $0<\alpha<1$, ordinary Green–Kubo scaling at $\alpha=1$ when $L$ tends to a positive constant, and zero-Green–Kubo antipersistence for $1<\alpha<2$. The cumulative formulation also survives at the critical endpoint. If

$$F(x)\sim b x^2L(1/x),\qquad H(x)=\int_1^xL(s)\frac{ds}{s}\longrightarrow\infty,$$

then, still without assuming a density,

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T)\sim4b\varepsilon^2H(T/\varepsilon).$$

For the proof, put $R=T/\varepsilon$ and $I(R)=\int_{(0,\infty)}(1-\cos(R\omega))\omega^{-2}\mu(d\omega)$. On $(0,R^{-1}]$, the inequality $1-\cos x\leq x^2/2$ gives an $O(L(R))$ contribution, while every interval bounded away from zero contributes $O(1)$. On $[R^{-1},\eta]$, Stieltjes integration by parts, followed by Dirichlet summation over the cosine half-periods, gives

$$I(R)=2b\int_{R^{-1}}^\eta L(1/\omega)\frac{d\omega}{\omega} +o(H(R))+O(L(R))+O(1).$$

Potter bounds make the remainder uniform as $R\to\infty$ and then $\eta\downarrow0$. Since the divergent borderline integral satisfies $L(R)=o(H(R))$, the last display is $2bH(R){1+o(1)\}$; substitution in the exact Fejér identity proves the factor $4b$. If a density exists with $f(\omega)\sim a\omega L(1/\omega)$, then $b=a/2$ and this reduces to the earlier $2a\varepsilon^2H(T/\varepsilon)$ law. The divergence of $H$ is essential: when it stays bounded, the global inverse spectral moment, not the local equivalent of $F$, determines the order-$\varepsilon^2$ coefficient.

A concrete failure under mere stationarity is $X_t=Z+Y_t$, where $Z$ is a centered random constant with variance $\sigma_0^2$ and $Y$ is an independent stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with covariance $e^{-|t|}$. Then

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) =\sigma_0^2T^2+2T\varepsilon -2\varepsilon^2\{1-e^{-T/\varepsilon}\}.$$

The regular spectral density of $Y$ still produces the usual Green–Kubo term, but it is lower order than the invariant random offset.

The frequency-domain statement is stronger than an eventual-sign covariance theorem. For example, add a flat spectral band $0.8\mathbf1_{[4,5]}(\omega)$ to the Gamma density $e^{-\omega}\omega^{0.7}/\Gamma(1.7)$. Its covariance is

$$\operatorname{Re}(1-it)^{-1.7} +0.8\frac{\sin(5t)-\sin(4t)}t.$$

The second term makes the covariance change sign forever and dominate its pointwise tail, but it is separated from frequency zero and contributes only $O(\varepsilon^2)$. The theorem still gives the sharp $\varepsilon^{1.7}$ variance. This is the continuous-time counterpart of the spectral regular-variation criterion for partial sums proved by Deligiannidis and Utev (2013).

A density-free benchmark puts mass $n^{-\alpha-1}$ at frequency $1/n$. Its cumulative mass satisfies

$$F(x)=\sum_{n\ge\lceil1/x\rceil}n^{-\alpha-1}\sim\frac{x^\alpha}{\alpha},$$

so the theorem applies although the measure is purely atomic. For $\alpha=1.3$, the certificate evaluates the exact Fejér sum using a direct finite sum and an independent Hurwitz-zeta tail. It finds order $1.300596$ and leading-constant ratio $1.000240$. The example also emphasizes that zero spectral mass at frequency zero is an observable-level averaging condition: atoms at nonzero frequencies need not disappear.

The same pure-point construction at the endpoint puts mass $n^{-3}$ at $1/n$. Then $F(x)\sim x^2/2$, so the critical theorem predicts $2\varepsilon^2\log(1/\varepsilon)$ at $T=1$. The direct Fejér sum and an independently refined Hurwitz-zeta tail agree within $2.71\times10^{-20}$. Regressing $\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon/\varepsilon^2$ against $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ gives slope $1.966971$ against the limiting value $2$; at the smallest tested $\varepsilon=2^{-20}$ the leading-term ratio is $1.025526$. The deliberately slow logarithmic convergence distinguishes this endpoint from the power-law cases.

Four covariance regimes

Integrable correlation

If $C$ is absolutely integrable and $K=\int_0^\infty C(s)ds$, dominated convergence gives, for fixed $T>0$,

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T)=2TK\,\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon).$$

This is the Green–Kubo regime used by the finite-chain pages. Exponential mixing is more than enough; a central limit theorem or a Poisson equation supplies the corresponding fluctuation limit.

Zero Green–Kubo and antipersistence

Integrability alone does not make the order-$\varepsilon$ coefficient nonzero. Suppose that $C$ is absolutely integrable,

$$K=\int_0^\infty C(s)ds=0,$$

and, for some $1<\alpha<2$,

$$C(t)\sim c\,t^{-\alpha}L(t),\qquad c\ne0,$$

with $L$ slowly varying and eventually positive. Karamata's tail theorem and its truncated-moment form give

$$\int_0^x C(s)ds\sim-\frac{c}{\alpha-1}x^{1-\alpha}L(x),\qquad \int_0^x sC(s)ds\sim\frac{c}{2-\alpha}x^{2-\alpha}L(x).$$

Substitution in the exact identity yields the cancellation theorem

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) \sim-\frac{2c}{(\alpha-1)(2-\alpha)} T^{2-\alpha}\varepsilon^\alpha L(T/\varepsilon).$$

Since the left side is nonnegative, a nonzero regularly varying tail under these assumptions must have $c<0$. Negative long-lag covariance cancels the short-lag contribution exactly enough to replace the usual $\varepsilon$ variance by $\varepsilon^\alpha L(1/\varepsilon)$. The fluctuation amplitude is therefore $\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}L(1/\varepsilon)^{1/2}$: smaller than $\sqrt\varepsilon$, but generally larger than $\varepsilon$.

A fully valid stationary Gaussian benchmark has spectral density proportional to $e^{-|\omega|}|\omega|^{\alpha-1}$. Its normalized covariance is

$$C_\alpha(t)=\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^\infty e^{-\omega}\omega^{\alpha-1}\cos(\omega t)d\omega =\operatorname{Re}(1-it)^{-\alpha}.$$

It has $K=0$ exactly and $C_\alpha(t)\sim\cos(\pi\alpha/2)t^{-\alpha}$, whose coefficient is negative for $1<\alpha<2$. Direct integration gives the exact finite-$\varepsilon$ formula

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) =\frac{2\varepsilon^2}{(\alpha-1)(2-\alpha)} \left\{\operatorname{Re}(1-iT/\varepsilon)^{2-\alpha}-1\right\}.$$

This is the continuous-time analogue of the antipersistent fractional noises introduced by Mandelbrot and Van Ness (1968). It also shows why the statement “integrable covariance implies an order-$\varepsilon$ correction” requires the qualification $K\ne0$.

The second critical index and the $\varepsilon^2$ boundary

The cancellation theorem has a second critical index at $\alpha=2$. Keep $K=0$ and suppose

$$C(t)\sim c\,t^{-2}L(t),\qquad c\ne0,$$

with $L$ slowly varying and eventually positive. Define

$$H(x)=\int_1^x\frac{L(s)}s\,ds.$$

If $H(x)\to\infty$, Karamata's tail theorem gives $\int_x^\infty C(s)ds\sim cL(x)/x$, while its critical truncated-moment form and $L(x)=o(H(x))$ give $\int_0^x sC(s)ds\sim cH(x)$. Hence

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) \sim-2c\,\varepsilon^2H(T/\varepsilon).$$

Again nonnegativity forces $c<0$. Thus a pure $t^{-2}$ tail produces $-2c\varepsilon^2\log(T/\varepsilon)$, not a pure $\varepsilon^2$ law.

If instead the first covariance moment is absolutely integrable,

$$M=\int_0^\infty sC(s)ds,\qquad \int_0^\infty s|C(s)|ds<\infty,$$

then $x\int_x^\infty|C(s)|ds\to0$ and the exact variance identity gives

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T)=-2M\varepsilon^2+o(\varepsilon^2).$$

For a nonzero stationary process the inequality is strict: $M<0$. To see this, use Bochner's spectral representation (1933)

$$C(t)=\int_{[0,\infty)}\cos(\omega t)\,\mu(d\omega),\qquad \mu\ge0.$$

Absolute integrability of $C$ makes $\mu$ absolutely continuous and therefore excludes a zero-frequency atom. For $B_R=\int_0^R X_tdt$, the covariance and spectral formulas give

$$\operatorname{Var}B_R\longrightarrow-2M,\qquad \operatorname{Var}B_R=2\int_{(0,\infty)} \frac{1-\cos(\omega R)}{\omega^2}\,\mu(d\omega).$$

Taking a Cesàro average in $R$, Fatou's lemma first proves integrability of $\omega^{-2}$ against $\mu$; dominated convergence then gives the exact identity

$$-M=\int_{(0,\infty)}\frac{\mu(d\omega)}{\omega^2}.$$

The right side is positive unless $\mu=0$, equivalently unless $X$ is zero in $L^2$. Thus the order-$\varepsilon^2$ coefficient cannot vanish under the stated assumptions. This includes regularly varying tails with $\alpha>2$, and the $\alpha=2$ case when $H(\infty)<\infty$.

The same Gamma-spectral benchmark makes both boundaries exact. At $\alpha=2$ and $\alpha>2$, respectively,

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) =\varepsilon^2\log\{1+(T/\varepsilon)^2\},\qquad \operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) \sim\frac{2\varepsilon^2}{(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}.$$

For the second formula, $M=-1/\{(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)\}$. Together with the $1<\alpha<2$ theorem, this is the complete zero-Green–Kubo trichotomy under the stated regular-variation or first-moment assumptions; it is distinct from the oscillatory periodic example below.

Long-range dependence

More generally, suppose that the covariance is eventually positive and regularly varying:

$$C(t)\sim c\,t^{-\alpha}L(t),\qquad c>0,\quad0<\alpha<1,$$

where $L$ is slowly varying. Karamata's integral theorem gives

$$\int_0^x C(s)ds\sim\frac{xC(x)}{1-\alpha},\qquad \int_0^x sC(s)ds\sim\frac{x^2C(x)}{2-\alpha}.$$

Substitution in the exact covariance identity, with $x=T/\varepsilon$, therefore gives

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T) \sim\frac{2c}{(1-\alpha)(2-\alpha)} T^{2-\alpha}\varepsilon^\alpha L(T/\varepsilon).$$

The Green–Kubo integral diverges and the fluctuation size is $\varepsilon^{\alpha/2}L(1/\varepsilon)^{1/2}$ rather than $\sqrt\varepsilon$. The pure-power formula is the special case $L\equiv1$.

The critical index is not one formula

At the boundary $C(t)\sim cL(t)/t$, put

$$H(x)=\int_1^x\frac{L(s)}s\,ds.$$

If $H(x)\to\infty$, then Karamata's theorem and $L(x)=o(H(x))$ give

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T)\sim2cT\varepsilon H(T/\varepsilon).$$

If $H(\infty)<\infty$, the tail is integrable and the ordinary Green–Kubo order $\varepsilon$ returns. Thus $C(t)\sim c/t$ gives $2cT\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon)$, but a logarithmic modifier changes even the critical scale. For $L(t)=(\log t)^\beta$,

$$\operatorname{Var}A_\varepsilon(T)\sim \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{2cT}{\beta+1}\varepsilon (\log(1/\varepsilon))^{\beta+1},&\beta>-1,\\[4pt] 2cT\varepsilon\log\log(1/\varepsilon),&\beta=-1, \end{cases}$$

whereas $\beta<-1$ is integrable. Therefore neither the coefficient nor even the power-and-log scale of the correction is universal.

The certificate tests both new scales on the valid covariance

$$C_\alpha(t)=\int_0^{e^{-1}}e^{-\lambda t}\lambda^{\alpha-1} \log(1/\lambda)\,d\lambda \sim\Gamma(\alpha)t^{-\alpha}\log t.$$

It is a positive mixture of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck covariances. Numerical integration of the exact finite-horizon variance gives asymptotic ratios $1.02166$ for $\alpha=0.4$ and $0.94394$ at the critical $\varepsilon\log^2(1/\varepsilon)$ scale.

For the invariant-atom counterexample, independent covariance quadrature agrees with the atom-plus-OU formula within $3.33\times10^{-16}$. At the smallest tested $\varepsilon$, the normalized variance is $0.370093896$ against the limiting atom mass $0.37$, while the residual after subtracting the atom is $0.999953050$ of its Green–Kubo leading term.

For the zero-Green–Kubo benchmark, independent covariance quadrature agrees with the exact formula within $1.1\times10^{-17}$. At $\alpha=1.4$, the measured order and asymptotic ratio are $1.399942$ and $0.999921$; at $\alpha=1.7$, they are $1.697433$ and $0.992367$. The boundary checks obtain the exact log-corrected ratio $1.000000000000$ at $\alpha=2$. At $\alpha=3$, independent quadrature gives $M=-0.5$, measured order $2.000000000$, and asymptotic ratio $0.999999999996$. Independent spectral quadrature gives $\int\omega^{-2}\mu(d\omega)=0.5$, agreeing with $-M$ within $1.1\times10^{-16}$.

The low-frequency certificate also reconciles the spectral and covariance-tail constants within $5.33\times10^{-15}$. For the oscillatory-band example above, independent covariance and spectral integration agree within $2.95\times10^{-17}$; the measured order is $1.697454$, the asymptotic ratio is $0.992431$, and the covariance changes sign 3,151 times on the deterministic mesh $20\le t\le2000$.

For the pure-point spectrum, the cumulative-mass ratio at $x=2^{-20}$ is $1.000000620$. Two independently truncated Fejér sums agree within $8.67\times10^{-19}$; the measured variance order and asymptotic ratio are $1.300596$ and $1.000240$.

For the pure-point critical spectrum, the cumulative-mass ratio is $1.000000954$, the independent summation discrepancy is $2.71\times10^{-20}$, the fitted logarithmic slope is $1.966971$, and the smallest-$\varepsilon$ leading-term ratio is $1.025526$.

A bounded positive volatility counterexample

For $0<\alpha<1$, let $G_t$ be a centered stationary Gaussian process with correlation

$$R(t)=(1+t^2)^{-\alpha/2}.$$

This is a valid covariance because it is a positive Gamma mixture of Gaussian kernels. Its spectral measure has no atoms, so the process is ergodic. Define a bounded strictly positive variance by

$$v_t=v_0+\delta\,\operatorname{sign}(G_t),\qquad0<\delta<v_0.$$

The Gaussian sign identity gives

$$C_v(t)=\frac{2\delta^2}{\pi}\arcsin R(t) \sim\frac{2\delta^2}{\pi}t^{-\alpha}.$$

Consequently, for integrated variance $V_\varepsilon(T)=\int_0^T v_{t/\varepsilon}dt$,

$$\operatorname{Var}V_\varepsilon(T) \sim\frac{4\delta^2}{\pi(1-\alpha)(2-\alpha)} T^{2-\alpha}\varepsilon^\alpha.$$

The observable is bounded and the driver is stationary and ergodic, yet the order-$\varepsilon$ Green–Kubo smile does not apply.

Implication for the uncorrelated volatility smile

When the Brownian return is independent of the variance driver, conditioning on integrated variance gives the Hull–White mixture $\mathbb E[C_{\rm BS}(V_\varepsilon)]$. For a smooth payoff, a second-order delta method gives

$$\mathbb E[C_{\rm BS}(V_\varepsilon)]-C_{\rm BS}(\mathbb EV_\varepsilon) =\tfrac12 C_{\rm BS}''(\mathbb EV_\varepsilon) \operatorname{Var}V_\varepsilon+o(\operatorname{Var}V_\varepsilon),$$

provided the normalized integrated variance has the required moment convergence. The sign-Gaussian example has Hermite rank one, and the long-range Gaussian limit theory supplies that normalization. Its convexity smile is therefore of order $\varepsilon^\alpha$, not $\varepsilon$.

This statement concerns the uncorrelated variance-mixture effect. Correlation between the stock and a fast diffusive factor can separately create an order-$\sqrt\varepsilon$ skew term; the two mechanisms should not be conflated.

Ergodic need not mean diffusive

Let $U$ be uniform on the circle and $Y_t=U+t/\varepsilon\pmod{2\pi}$. The continuous flow is stationary and ergodic. With $v(y)=v_0+\delta\cos y>0$,

$$\int_0^T v(Y_t)dt=v_0T+\delta\varepsilon [\sin(U+T/\varepsilon)-\sin U],$$

and hence exactly

$$\operatorname{Var}\left(\int_0^T v(Y_t)dt\right) =\delta^2\varepsilon^2[1-\cos(T/\varepsilon)].$$

The correction is order $\varepsilon^2$ with an oscillatory coefficient. The one-sided covariance integral does not converge. This example is smooth, bounded and positive, so stronger mixing or fluctuation assumptions really are necessary.

Scope and literature

The theorems above determine the variance scale from either the covariance tail or the low-frequency spectral density, together with whether the Green–Kubo integral vanishes; they do not claim a universal option expansion for every long-memory process or nonsmooth payoff. Distributional limits depend on the process and on the Hermite rank of the observable. The regular-variation step is Karamata's integral theorem (1933). Primary long-memory references are Taqqu (1975), Dobrushin and Major (1979), and Taqqu (1979). In finance, Comte and Renault (1998) develop continuous-time long-memory stochastic volatility.

Sircar and Papanicolaou's fast-volatility calculation invokes a fluctuation limit in addition to ergodicity. The finite-state chain used on this site satisfies the stronger assumptions; the counterexamples delimit the generality of the claim rather than contradicting the chain calculation.
