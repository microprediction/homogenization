What a fast-switching yield curve identifies

A first-order identifiability theorem, exact loading and initial-mixture rank boundaries, and an explicit finite-rate remainder.

Research note, 23 September 2026. This note develops the three-number example; the asymptotic statements concern a fixed finite maturity interval as the generator is sped up.

Model and convention

Let $Q_0$ be an irreducible generator on $n$ states, with stationary row vector $\pi$, and let the regime generator be $mQ_0$, $m\to\infty$. The short rate obeys $dr_t=\kappa(\theta_{y_t}-r_t)dt+\sqrt{s_{y_t}}\,dW_t$, with $\kappa>0$, $s_i\ge0$, and $y_t$ independent of $W$. Define

$$B(T)=\frac{1-e^{-\kappa T}}{\kappa},\quad I_j(T)=\int_0^T B(t)^jdt,\quad u=\theta-(\pi\theta){\bf1},\quad v=s-(\pi s){\bf1}.$$

For a known initial rate $x$, write $P_i(T)=e^{-B(T)x}a_i(T)$ for a specified starting regime and $P_\pi(T)=e^{-B(T)x}\pi a(T)$ for a stationary start. The exact finite-dimensional equation is

$$a'(T)=\{mQ_0+\operatorname{diag}g(T)\}a(T),\quad a(0)={\bf1}, \qquad g(T)=-\kappa\theta B(T)+\tfrac12 s B(T)^2.$$

In particular, $g(0)=0$. Put $R=I-{\bf1}\pi$ and $H=-Q_0^{\#} =\int_0^\infty e^{Q_0t}R\,dt$. The symmetric Green–Kubo form is $\mathcal K_0^{\rm sym}(f,h) =\frac12\{\pi[f(Hh)]+\pi[h(Hf)]\}$ on centered vectors. The coefficients below are computed with $Q_0$; their actual contribution at rate $mQ_0$ is divided by $m$.

Theorem: the first-order information

$$\begin{aligned} \log P_\pi(T)+B(T)x &=\beta_1 I_1+\beta_2 I_2+\beta_3 I_3+\beta_4 I_4+E_\pi(T),\\ \beta_1&=-\kappa\bar\theta,\qquad \beta_2=\tfrac12\bar s+\frac{\kappa^2}{m}\mathcal K_0(u,u),\\ \beta_3&=-\frac{\kappa}{m}\mathcal K_0^{\rm sym}(u,v),\qquad \beta_4=\frac{1}{4m}\mathcal K_0(v,v). \end{aligned}$$

For every fixed $L<\infty$, $\sup_{0\le T\le L}|E_\pi(T)|=O_L(m^{-2})$. With $\kappa$ and $x$ fixed, the four functions $I_1,\ldots,I_4$ are linearly independent on every open maturity interval. Hence an ideal first-order stationary curve uniquely determines these four coefficients. It does not separately determine $\bar s$ and $\mathcal K_0(u,u)/m$ from $\beta_2$, nor the underlying chain. Two chains can share all four first-order coefficients and have different exact curves at order $m^{-2}$. Finite-rate observations do not recover these coefficients without error; a bound appears below.

Let $c_2=\kappa^2\mathcal K_0(u,u)/m$, $c_3=\beta_3$ and $c_4=\beta_4$. The $2\times2$ Green–Kubo matrix is positive semidefinite, so $c_3^2\le4c_2c_4$, and the observable restriction is $\beta_3^2\le4\beta_2\beta_4$. When $\beta_4=0$, necessarily $\beta_3=0$. Equality for the latent $c$'s holds with two regimes, including the degenerate zero-variance case. Strict inequality for the observable $\beta$'s alone cannot establish three regimes: $\bar s>0$ makes it strict even for two states. If $\bar s$ is separately known, strict inequality among the $c$'s implies at least three regimes. It still does not identify their transition graph.

The general fixed-feature Gram theorem

The two-feature restriction is a special case. Suppose the centered forcing separates as

$$g_i(t)-\pi g(t)=\sum_{r=1}^d\phi_r(t)f_r(i),\qquad \pi f_r=0,$$

and let $F=[f_1\ \cdots\ f_d]$ be the $n\times d$ feature matrix. Define the fixed-parameter Green–Kubo Gram matrix

$$G_{rs}=\mathcal K_0^{\rm sym}(f_r,f_s).$$

Then the stationary first correction is

$$\frac1m\int_0^T\phi(t)^\top G\phi(t)\,dt,$$

and, for every irreducible finite chain,

$$G\succeq0,\qquad \ker G=\ker F,\qquad \operatorname{rank}G=\operatorname{rank}F\le\min(d,n-1).$$

The equality of kernels is stronger than the rank upper bound. For any coefficient vector $a$, $a^\top Ga=\mathcal K_0^{\rm sym}(Fa,Fa)$. The additive-functional martingale argument in the proof below makes this zero exactly when the centered state function $Fa$ is zero. Thus an identified fixed-feature rank $r$ requires at least $r+1$ regimes. It does not identify the generator: many chains and feature embeddings produce the same $G$.

Identification of the whole matrix still requires the maturity functions $\int_0^T\phi_r(t)\phi_s(t)\,dt$ to be linearly independent over the observed interval. Without that design condition, a curve identifies only the corresponding linear combinations of the entries of $G$.

The exact finite-horizon covariance

The Green–Kubo matrix is a covariance rate, not the covariance at a finite horizon. Let $Y$ start in stationarity, let its generator be $mQ_0$, put $H=-Q_0^\#$, and define

$$Z_T=\int_0^T F^\top e_{Y_s}\,ds,\qquad J_m(T)=\frac{T}{m}H-\frac1{m^2}H^2(I-e^{mQ_0T}).$$

Here the columns of $F$ are centered and $e_i$ is the state basis vector. Direct integration of the two-time covariance gives the exact identity

$$\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(Z_T) =F^\top\operatorname{diag}(\pi)J_m(T)F +F^\top J_m(T)^\top\operatorname{diag}(\pi)F =\frac{2T}{m}G+O(m^{-2}).$$

The remainder is uniform over every $T\ge0$: exponential mixing bounds $H^2(I-e^{mQ_0T})$ independently of $T$. For each fixed $T>0$ the exponentially small part also yields the explicit boundary correction

$$\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(Z_T) =\frac{2T}{m}G-\frac1{m^2} \left[F^\top\operatorname{diag}(\pi)H^2F +F^\top(H^2)^\top\operatorname{diag}(\pi)F\right]+O(e^{-m\delta T}/m^2).$$

At every finite $T>0$ this exact covariance is positive semidefinite and has $\ker\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(Z_T)=\ker F$. The rank statement is stronger than the closed stationary formula: it remains true for every initial regime prior $\nu$, including a deterministic start, although the covariance formula itself then changes.

To prove this, suppose the variance of $\int_0^T f(Y_s)ds$ vanishes and choose a state $i_0$ in the support of $\nu$. The integral is constant almost surely. On the no-jump path it equals $Tf_{i_0}$. For any directed path $i_0\to i_1\to\cdots\to i_k$, consider paths that make exactly these $k$ jumps and then no more before $T$. Their ordered jump times have a strictly positive density on the open simplex. The integral is the continuous affine function $f_{i_0}t_1+\cdots+f_{i_{k-1}}(t_k-t_{k-1})+f_{i_k}(T-t_k)$. Since it is almost surely constant, it is constant throughout that simplex, forcing $f_{i_{r-1}}=f_{i_r}$ for every edge. Irreducibility gives a directed path from $i_0$ to every state, so $f$ is constant; after centering, $f=0$. Hence for arbitrary $\nu$

$$\ker\operatorname{Cov}_\nu(Z_T)=\ker F,\qquad \operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Cov}_\nu(Z_T)=\operatorname{rank}F.$$

This still says nothing about rank after different time-dependent loadings have been integrated. For a stationary start and reversible $Q_0$, spectral calculus also gives the Loewner bound $0\preceq\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(Z_T)\preceq2TG/m$; neither the closed formula nor that upper bound is asserted for a nonstationary start.

The certificate independently integrates the joint first- and second-moment equations. Across 48 dense nonreversible chain/prior cases, including point-mass starts, every numerical covariance has the predicted rank; under stationarity the ODE and closed formula agree within $1.6\times10^{-14}$.

Why an integrated rank is not a regime count

The rank statement applies to one fixed feature matrix. If $p$ contracts have a maturity-dependent loading matrix $L(t)\in\mathbb R^{p\times d}$, their integrated coefficient matrix is

$$D(T)=\int_0^T L(t)G L(t)^\top dt.$$

Let $r=\operatorname{rank}G$ and choose any full-column factor $G=RR^\top$ with $R\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$. Then for every $x\in\mathbb R^p$,

$$x^\top D(T)x=\int_0^T\|R^\top L(t)^\top x\|^2dt.$$

Consequently, writing essential span to ignore measure-zero values of a merely measurable loading,

$$\ker D(T)=\{x:R^\top L(t)^\top x=0\text{ for a.e. }t\},\qquad \operatorname{rank}D(T)=\dim\operatorname*{ess\,span}_{t\in[0,T]}\operatorname{range}(L(t)R).$$

For continuous loadings, “almost everywhere” can be replaced by “everywhere.” This is the exact rank boundary: integrating does not recover another property of the hidden chain; it measures the span swept out by the loaded fixed-feature range.

A useful finite-dimensional specialization makes the possible inflation explicit. If

$$L(t)=\sum_{j=1}^q\psi_j(t)L_j,\qquad \Gamma_{ij}=\int_0^T\psi_i(t)\psi_j(t)dt\succ0,$$

and $\mathcal B=[L_1R\ \cdots\ L_qR]$, then

$$D(T)=\mathcal B(\Gamma\otimes I_r)\mathcal B^\top,\qquad \operatorname{rank}D(T)=\operatorname{rank}\mathcal B\le\min(p,qr).$$

The matrix $D(T)$ is a finite-horizon controllability Gramian with input matrix $L(t)R$; the rank interpretation goes back to Kalman (1960).

Although every integrand has rank at most $r$, its range can rotate with $t$, so $D(T)$ can attain the larger bound $qr$. The smallest counterexample uses only two regimes and one centered feature. Take scalar $G=[g]$ with $g>0$ and $L(t)=(1,t,t^2)^\top$. At $T=1$,

$$D(1)=g\begin{pmatrix} 1&1/2&1/3\\ 1/2&1/3&1/4\\ 1/3&1/4&1/5 \end{pmatrix},$$

the positive-definite $3\times3$ Hilbert matrix. Hence $\operatorname{rank}D(1)=3$ even though $n-1=1$. A rank lower bound on the number of regimes is valid for the fixed Green–Kubo Gram matrix after known loadings have been removed, not for a matrix obtained by integrating heterogeneous maturity loadings. The certificate also checks a rank-two, three-basis example attaining rank six and reproduces the factorization by independent quadrature within $2\times10^{-12}$. This is the same distinction needed in multi-name credit and multi-factor term-structure applications.

Known starting regime

For any specified state $i$, the same theorem adds two unintegrated terms:

$$\log P_i(T)+B(T)x =\sum_{j=1}^4\beta_j I_j(T) +\frac1m\left[-\kappa(Hu)_iB(T)+\tfrac12(Hv)_iB(T)^2\right] +E_i(T),\qquad \sup_{0\le T\le L}|E_i(T)|=O_L(m^{-2}).$$

The coefficients of $B$ and $B^2$ record the Poisson solutions for the initial state. Their $\pi$-weighted averages are zero, recovering the stationary formula. They are two distinct functions, but they do not contribute two new independent shapes to a single curve. The identities

$$B(T)=T-\kappa I_1(T),\qquad B(T)^2=2I_1(T)-2\kappa I_2(T)$$

show that the six displayed functions span only $\{T,I_1,I_2,I_3,I_4\}$, a five-dimensional space. Writing the known-start coefficients as $\gamma_{1i}=-\kappa(Hu)_i/m$ and $\gamma_{2i}=(Hv)_i/(2m)$, one curve determines only

$$(\gamma_{1i},\;\beta_1-\kappa\gamma_{1i}+2\gamma_{2i},\; \beta_2-2\kappa\gamma_{2i},\;\beta_3,\;\beta_4)$$

at this order. If separately labeled curves for several initial regimes are available, their differences isolate $(\gamma_{1i}-\gamma_{1j})B+ (\gamma_{2i}-\gamma_{2j})B^2$. Both relative coefficients can then be read from a maturity interval; a stationary-start curve also isolates the common $\beta$'s.

The approximation has a uniform value error but no claimed uniform derivative error near $T=0$. In particular, differentiating its known-start term at zero would give a spurious $O(m^{-1})$ initial slope; the order-$m^{-2}$ initial layer cancels it in a window of width $O(m^{-1})$.

An unobserved initial mixture: outer and boundary information

Let the initial regime have an unknown row distribution $\nu$. Averaging the statewise expansion before taking its logarithm gives, uniformly on a fixed bounded maturity interval,

$$\log P_\nu(T)+B(T)x =\sum_{j=1}^4\beta_jI_j(T) +\frac1m\left[-\kappa\nu Hu\,B(T)+\tfrac12\nu Hv\,B(T)^2\right] +O_L(m^{-2}).$$

Thus the fixed-maturity outer expansion sees the unknown mixture only through $\nu H[u\ v]$. If the common stationary coefficients are known separately, the whole distribution $\nu$ is identified at this order exactly when $\operatorname{rank}[u\ v]=n-1$; otherwise any zero-sum row vector in the kernel of $H[u\ v]$ is an observationally equivalent perturbation (while it remains inside the probability simplex). Without a stationary baseline, the shape aliases in the preceding section further mix these projections with $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$.

Exact transient observability

The underlying rank boundary is clearest for a general centered feature matrix $F\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$. For $Z_T=\int_0^T F^\top e_{Y_s}ds$ and any initial distribution $\nu$, direct semigroup integration gives the exact mean

$$\mathbb E_\nu Z_T =\frac1m\nu H(I-e^{mQ_0T})F.$$

At maturities $T_1,\ldots,T_q$, define the transient observability matrix

$$\mathcal O_m=[H(I-e^{mQ_0T_1})F\ \cdots\ H(I-e^{mQ_0T_q})F].$$

Two priors $\nu,\nu'$ have the same mean responses exactly when $(\nu-\nu')\mathcal O_m=0$. Consequently the responses identify every prior if and only if

$$\operatorname{rank}[{\bf1}\ \mathcal O_m]=n, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \operatorname{rank}\mathcal O_m=n-1.$$

This is the observability counterpart of Kalman's 1960 rank framework. It concerns initial-distribution information, not the fixed-feature Green–Kubo rank and not the rank created by maturity-dependent contract loadings.

The whole maturity curve: an exact Krylov criterion

The finite list above leaves open whether some choice of scaled maturities can identify the prior. This has an intrinsic answer. For $\tau>0$ put $R(\tau)=H(I-e^{Q_0\tau})F$. Then

$$\operatorname*{span}_{\tau>0}\operatorname{range}R(\tau) =\operatorname{range}[F\ Q_0F\ \cdots\ Q_0^{n-2}F].$$

Consequently the complete scaled-maturity mean curve identifies every initial distribution if and only if

$$\operatorname{rank}[{\bf1}\ F\ Q_0F\ \cdots\ Q_0^{n-2}F]=n.$$

This is the classical Kalman reachability condition on the centered state space, used here through its observability dual. Equivalently, the PBH obstruction is a nonzero left eigenvector $\ell Q_0=\lambda\ell$, $\lambda\ne0$, with $\ell F=0$; see Hautus (1969). For a reversible chain and one scalar feature, full identification therefore requires every nonzero eigenvalue to be simple and the feature to have a nonzero component in every nonconstant eigenmode. A repeated eigenspace is a genuine obstruction: varying maturity cannot create more than one direction from one vector inside that eigenspace.

Reversibility is sufficient for a real spectrum, but neither reversibility nor diagonalizability is necessary for the all-maturity conclusion. Suppose the restriction of $Q_0$ to the centered state space has only real eigenvalues, repeated eigenvalues and Jordan blocks being allowed, and a centered scalar feature $F$ is cyclic:

$$\operatorname{rank}[{\bf1}\ F\ Q_0F\ \cdots\ Q_0^{r-1}F]=n, \qquad r=n-1.$$

Then every ordered tuple $0<\tau_1<\cdots<\tau_r$ identifies the prior:

$$\det[ {\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ \cdots\ R(\tau_r)]\ne0.$$

Indeed, if a nonzero centered row vector $\ell$ annihilated all response columns, $h(t)=\ell R(t)$ would have the $r+1$ distinct zeros $0,\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_r$. Cyclicity makes $h'(t)=\ell e^{Q_0t}F$ nonzero. In a real Jordan basis this derivative is an exponential polynomial

$$h'(t)=\sum_{q=1}^s p_q(t)e^{-a_qt}, \qquad \deg p_q<m_q,\qquad \sum_qm_q=r.$$

These functions form an extended Chebyshev system, so a nonzero $h'$ has at most $r-1$ real zeros counting multiplicity. Rolle's theorem would give at least $r$, a contradiction. Cyclicity also explains exactly which repetitions are permitted: it forces one Jordan chain per eigenvalue. A repeated eigenspace with two independent eigenvectors remains an obstruction to any scalar feature.

For a simple spectrum the theorem has an explicit determinant. Write $0,-a_1,\ldots,-a_r$, with $0<a_1<\cdots<a_r$, choose a real right-eigenvector basis $\psi_0={\bf1},\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_r$, and expand $F=\sum_{k=1}^r f_k\psi_k$. Cyclicity is then equivalent to every $f_k\ne0$, and

$$\begin{aligned} \det[ {\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ \cdots\ R(\tau_r)] &=\det[\psi_0\ \cdots\ \psi_r] \left(\prod_{k=1}^r{f_k\over a_k}\right) \det[1-e^{-a_k\tau_j}]_{k,j=1}^r,\\ \operatorname{sgn}\det[1-e^{-a_k\tau_j}]_{k,j=1}^r &=(-1)^{r(r-1)/2}, \end{aligned}$$

The sign follows by continuity and the small-maturity exponential Vandermonde limit. The ordinary and confluent cases are the classical exponential Chebyshev/total-positivity mechanism developed by Schoenberg (1947).

Sharp conditioning when all maturities move to the long end

Exact rank at every finite maturity does not imply a uniformly stable inverse. The simple real-spectrum case above gives the sharp loss. Fix distinct offsets $c_1<\cdots<c_r$ and translate the entire scaled-maturity design by $M$:

$$A_M=[{\bf1}\ R(M+c_1)\ \cdots\ R(M+c_r)].$$

Order its singular values as $\sigma_1(A_M)\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_n(A_M)$. If $0<a_1<\cdots<a_r$, the scalar feature is cyclic, and $M+c_1>0$, then

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_1(A_M),\sigma_2(A_M)&=\Theta(1),\\ \sigma_{j+2}(A_M)&=\Theta(e^{-a_jM}), &&j=1,\ldots,r-1,\\ |\det A_M|&=\Theta\!\left( e^{-M(a_1+\cdots+a_{r-1})}\right),\\ \kappa_2(A_M)&=\Theta(e^{a_{r-1}M}). \end{aligned}$$

Thus all $r$ distinct maturities retain exact rank for every finite $M$, while the weakest prior direction vanishes at the second-fastest decay rate. The fastest rate $a_r$ does not enter the leading determinant.

To see why, use the eigenbasis and remove the fixed nonzero modal factors. The response block becomes $B_M=[1-e^{-a_k(M+c_j)}]_{k,j=1}^r$. Keep its first column and subtract it from every other column. With $D_M=\operatorname{diag}(e^{-a_1M},\ldots,e^{-a_rM})$, the result is

$$\left[\,{\bf1}-D_Mz_1\ \middle|\ D_M(z_1-z_2)\ \middle|\ \cdots\ \middle|\ D_M(z_1-z_r)\,\right], \qquad (z_j)_k=e^{-a_kc_j}.$$

The exponential Chebyshev property makes every required leading minor of the difference block nonzero. Exterior powers then have successive orders $1,e^{-a_1M},e^{-M(a_1+a_2)},\ldots, e^{-M(a_1+\cdots+a_{r-1})}$, which gives the singular-value hierarchy by taking consecutive ratios. In the determinant the unscaled common column uses the fastest row, leaving precisely the $r-1$ slower rates. Fixed eigenvector and column transformations change constants, not these exponents.

A nonreversible four-state certificate has rates $(0.43537897,1.02585656,2.13876447)$. Translating offsets $(0.2,0.8,1.5)$ gives measured augmented singular-value rates $(-0.00130,0,0.43609,1.02644)$, determinant rate $1.46123$, and condition-number rate $1.02775$, against predictions $(0,0,0.43538,1.02586)$, $1.46124$, and $1.02586$. This result concerns transient initial-mixture observability across maturities; it is not a claim about fixed-feature Green–Kubo rank or rank created by integrating different contract loadings.

Gaussian quote noise: exact rank is not statistical recovery

The preceding condition number has a direct minimax consequence. Let $C_M=[R(M+c_1)\ \cdots\ R(M+c_r)]$, let $\mathcal H_0=\{h:h^\top\mathbf1=0\}$, and define the weakest prior signal

$$s_M=\min_{h\in\mathcal H_0,\ \|h\|_2=1}\|C_M^\top h\|_2.$$

Under the simple real-spectrum and cyclicity hypotheses of the long-end theorem,

$$s_M=\Theta(e^{-a_{r-1}M}).$$

Indeed, choose an orthonormal basis $U$ of $\mathcal H_0$. Orthogonally reducing $[\mathbf1\ C_M]$ and eliminating its bounded normalization row turns it, through uniformly bounded invertible transformations, into a block matrix with lower block $U^\top C_M$. Hence its least singular value and $s_M$ have the same order.

Now idealize noisy curve measurements as

$$Y=C_M^\top\nu+\sigma Z,\qquad Z\sim N(0,I_r),$$

where $\nu$ is an unknown initial distribution and $\sigma$ is a quote-noise scale. Fix $0<\rho\le\min_i\pi_i$, choose a unit minimizer $h_M\in\mathcal H_0$, and put $\nu_\pm=\pi\pm\rho h_M$. Both are probability vectors because $\|h_M\|_\infty\le1$. For every estimator $\widehat\nu(Y)$, the exact Gaussian two-point testing calculation gives

$$\begin{aligned} \max_{\pm}\Pr_{\nu_\pm}\{\|\widehat\nu-\nu_\pm\|_2\ge\rho\} &\ge \Phi\!\left(-{\rho s_M\over\sigma}\right),\\ \max_{\pm}\mathbb E_{\nu_\pm}\|\widehat\nu-\nu_\pm\|_2^2 &\ge \rho^2\Phi\!\left(-{\rho s_M\over\sigma}\right). \end{aligned}$$

The two Gaussian means are separated by $2\rho s_M$, so their optimal equal-prior classification error is exactly the displayed normal tail. Any estimator with error below $\rho$ induces the correct nearest-prior classifier, proving both bounds. Thus fixed noise makes the lower bounds tend to $1/2$ and $\rho^2/2$ as the design moves right. To make the first probability smaller than a fixed $\delta<1/2$, it is necessary that

$$\sigma\le {\rho s_M\over\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta)} =O(e^{-a_{r-1}M}).$$

This is a local two-prior lower bound for the stated Gaussian observation model, not a claim about market microstructure or a change to noiseless identifiability. It is the finite-dimensional two-point method originating in Le Cam (1973).

In the four-state certificate, the restricted least-singular-value rate is $1.02644$ against $a_{r-1}=1.02586$. With $\rho=0.1$ and $\sigma=10^{-5}$, the exact testing lower bound rises from $0.0709$ at $M=4$ to $0.499998$ at $M=16$. A Gaussian simulation with 200,000 draws per prior at $M=4$ agrees with the exact testing error within $0.002$.

The exponential threshold is also sufficient. Let $U$ have orthonormal columns spanning $\mathcal H_0$, put $A_M=C_M^\top U$, and let $\Pi_\Delta$ denote Euclidean projection onto the probability simplex. Since $A_M$ is nonsingular, define the projected inverse estimator

$$\widetilde\nu=\pi+UA_M^{-1}(Y-C_M^\top\pi),\qquad \widehat\nu=\Pi_\Delta(\widetilde\nu).$$

For every true prior $\nu\in\Delta$, exact inversion and nonexpansiveness of metric projection relative to points in $\Delta$ give

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\nu\|\widehat\nu-\nu\|_2^2 &\le\sigma^2\|A_M^{-1}\|_F^2 \le {r\sigma^2\over s_M^2},\\ \Pr_\nu\{\|\widehat\nu-\nu\|_2\ge\rho\} &\le\Pr\{\chi_r\ge\rho s_M/\sigma\}. \end{aligned}$$

Here $s_M=\sigma_{\min}(A_M)$ and $\chi_r$ is the Euclidean norm of an $r$-dimensional standard Gaussian. If $q_{r,1-\delta}$ is its $(1-\delta)$ quantile, then

$$\sigma\le {\rho s_M\over q_{r,1-\delta}}$$

is sufficient for error probability at most $\delta$, uniformly over the entire simplex. Together with the two-prior converse, this proves that the noise scale $s_M=\Theta(e^{-a_{r-1}M})$ is sharp: uniform consistent recovery along a translated long-end design is possible if $\sigma_M/s_M\to0$ and impossible if that ratio stays bounded away from zero along a subsequence.

At $M=4$ the certificate directly inverts 200,000 Gaussian observations. The empirical unconstrained mean-square error agrees with the exact trace formula within one percent; simplex projection never increases the samplewise error, and the empirical projected tail lies below the displayed $\chi_3$ bound.

Correlated quote noise: whitened observability

Independence and equal quote variances are not essential. Let $\Gamma_M$ be a known positive-definite covariance across the $r$ maturity quotes and consider

$$Y=C_M^\top\nu+\sigma\Gamma_M^{1/2}Z, \qquad Z\sim N(0,I_r).$$

With $U$ as above, put

$$B_M=\Gamma_M^{-1/2}C_M^\top U, \qquad s_{M,\Gamma}=\sigma_{\min}(B_M).$$

The entire two-point argument holds in this whitened geometry. Choosing a right singular vector of $B_M$ for its weakest direction gives, for every estimator,

$$\begin{aligned} \max_{\pm}\Pr_{\nu_\pm} \{\|\widehat\nu-\nu_\pm\|_2\ge\rho\} &\ge\Phi\!\left(-{\rho s_{M,\Gamma}\over\sigma}\right),\\ \max_{\pm}\mathbb E_{\nu_\pm} \|\widehat\nu-\nu_\pm\|_2^2 &\ge\rho^2\Phi\!\left( -{\rho s_{M,\Gamma}\over\sigma}\right). \end{aligned}$$

Conversely, the generalized least-squares coordinate estimator

$$\widehat x=B_M^\dagger\Gamma_M^{-1/2} (Y-C_M^\top\pi),\qquad \widehat\nu=\Pi_\Delta(\pi+U\widehat x)$$

satisfies the matching bounds

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb E_\nu\|\widehat\nu-\nu\|_2^2 &\le \sigma^2\operatorname{tr} \{(B_M^\top B_M)^{-1}\} \le {r\sigma^2\over s_{M,\Gamma}^2},\\ \Pr_\nu\{\|\widehat\nu-\nu\|_2\ge\rho\} &\le\Pr\{\chi_r\ge\rho s_{M,\Gamma}/\sigma\}. \end{aligned}$$

Thus $s_{M,\Gamma}$, not the unweighted condition number, is the exact noise scale. If constants $0<\gamma_-\le\gamma_+<\infty$ satisfy $\gamma_-I\preceq\Gamma_M\preceq\gamma_+I$ uniformly in $M$, then

$$ {s_M\over\sqrt{\gamma_+}} \le s_{M,\Gamma}\le {s_M\over\sqrt{\gamma_-}},$$

so the sharp exponent remains $a_{r-1}$. No such conclusion is made when the quote covariance itself becomes ill-conditioned with maturity: in that case its eigenvalue scale is part of the inverse problem. Whitening and generalized least squares go back to Aitken's original treatment; here the calculation is finite dimensional and exact because $\Gamma_M$ is assumed known.

The certificate uses a correlated, heteroskedastic three-quote covariance with eigenvalues $(0.3846,0.6088,2.6066)$. Its whitened weakest-signal decay rate is $1.02644$, against $a_{r-1}=1.02586$. At $M=4$, 200,000 Gaussian draws reproduce the exact two-prior testing error and generalized least-squares mean-square error within $0.002$ and one percent respectively; simplex projection again obeys the $\chi_3$ tail bound.

Deterministic quote-error balls: the same sharp scale

The singular-value threshold is not an artifact of Gaussian tails. Consider the adversarial observation model

$$Y=C_M^\top\nu+e,\qquad \|\Gamma_M^{-1/2}e\|_2\le\delta,$$

with no probability law for $e$. Keep the definitions $B_M=\Gamma_M^{-1/2}C_M^\top U$ and $s_{M,\Gamma}=\sigma_{\min}(B_M)>0$. The projected generalized inverse

$$\widehat x=B_M^\dagger\Gamma_M^{-1/2} (Y-C_M^\top\pi),\qquad \widehat\nu=\Pi_\Delta(\pi+U\widehat x)$$

obeys the deterministic, uniform bound

$$\sup_{\nu\in\Delta}\ \sup_{\|\Gamma_M^{-1/2}e\|_2\le\delta} \|\widehat\nu-\nu\|_2 \le {\delta\over s_{M,\Gamma}}.$$

To see this, write $\nu=\pi+Ux$. Full column rank gives $\widehat x-x=B_M^\dagger\Gamma_M^{-1/2}e$; the operator norm of $B_M^\dagger$ is $1/s_{M,\Gamma}$, $U$ is an isometry, and projection onto the simplex cannot increase distance from the true $\nu\in\Delta$.

The scale is also necessary, with no constants hidden. Fix $0<\rho\le\min_i\pi_i$ and let $x_M$ be a unit right singular vector for $s_{M,\Gamma}$, $h_M=Ux_M$, and $\nu_\pm=\pi\pm\rho h_M$. If $\delta\ge\rho s_{M,\Gamma}$, the two admissible observation sets intersect at $y_*=C_M^\top\pi$: the errors $e_\pm=y_*-C_M^\top\nu_\pm$ have whitened norm exactly $\rho s_{M,\Gamma}$. Since $\|\nu_+-\nu_-\|_2=2\rho$, every estimator evaluated at $y_*$ is at distance at least $\rho$ from one of the two priors. Therefore

$$\inf_{\widehat\nu}\ \sup_{\nu\in\Delta}\ \sup_{\|\Gamma_M^{-1/2}e\|_2\le\delta} \|\widehat\nu(Y)-\nu\|_2\ge\rho \quad\text{whenever}\quad \delta\ge\rho s_{M,\Gamma}.$$

A local parameter ball makes the whole phase transition exact, not only its threshold. Define

$$\Theta_\rho=\{\pi+Ux:\|x\|_2\le\rho\}, \qquad 0<\rho\le\min_i\pi_i.$$

The displayed restriction on $\rho$ is sufficient for $\Theta_\rho\subset\Delta$, since every row of the isometry $U$ has norm at most one. For estimators allowed to depend on the known radii $\rho$ and $\delta$, the deterministic local minimax risk is exactly

$$\begin{aligned} R^*_{M,\Gamma}(\rho,\delta) &:=\inf_{\widehat\nu}\ \sup_{\nu\in\Theta_\rho}\ \sup_{\|\Gamma_M^{-1/2}e\|_2\le\delta} \|\widehat\nu(C_M^\top\nu+e)-\nu\|_2\\ &=\min\!\left\{\rho,{\delta\over s_{M,\Gamma}}\right\}. \end{aligned}$$

For the upper bound, use the constant estimator $\pi$ when $\delta\ge\rho s_{M,\Gamma}$. Below that threshold, apply the generalized inverse and project onto the convex set $\Theta_\rho$; metric projection cannot increase its distance from the true point, so its error is at most $\delta/s_{M,\Gamma}$. For the converse, put $a=\min\{\rho,\delta/s_{M,\Gamma}\}$ and replace $\rho$ by $a$ in the two weakest-direction priors above. Their error balls meet at $C_M^\top\pi$, and the triangle inequality forces loss at least $a$ for one of them. This proves the identity at every noise radius, including the saturation regime.

Thus uniform robust consistency holds when $\delta_M/s_{M,\Gamma}\to0$ and fails whenever $\delta_M\ge\rho s_{M,\Gamma}$ along a subsequence for some fixed feasible $\rho$. Under the uniformly bounded covariance hypothesis above, the real-spectrum long-end tolerance is again $\Theta(e^{-a_{r-1}M})$. This is a worst-case deterministic statement, distinct from the preceding Gaussian probability statements. The comparison belongs to the optimal-recovery/statistical-estimation correspondence developed by Donoho (1994); the constants here are exact because the finite-dimensional ellipsoid is resolved directly by the SVD.

For the correlated four-state certificate at $M=4$, the two adversarial Mahalanobis balls touch exactly at the common midpoint. Direct generalized inversion attains $\delta/s_{M,\Gamma}$ in the weakest left-singular direction, and 10,000 errors sampled throughout a smaller ball all satisfy the uniform bound. The local-ball certificate also checks six noise-to-threshold ratios from zero to three: the two-point lower certificate and the projected-inverse/constant upper certificate both equal $\rho\min\{1,\delta/(\rho s_{M,\Gamma})\}$.

Oscillatory long-end converse: infinitely many translated aliases

The real-spectrum hypothesis is essential for a uniform long-end lower bound. In three states, suppose the centered eigenvalues are $-a\pm ib$, with $a,b>0$, and the scalar feature is cyclic. Fix any offsets $c_1<c_2$, and write

$$D(M)=\det[\,\mathbf1\ R(M+c_1)\ R(M+c_2)\,].$$

Substitution in the exact aliasing kernel below gives, for a nonzero model-dependent constant $C_0$, the identity

$$\begin{aligned} D(M)&=C_0e^{-aM}\{L(M)+Ce^{-aM}\},\\ L(M)&=e^{-ac_1}\sin\{b(M+c_1)\} -e^{-ac_2}\sin\{b(M+c_2)\},\\ C&=e^{-a(c_1+c_2)}\sin\{b(c_2-c_1)\}. \end{aligned}$$

The leading term is a nonzero sinusoid: $L(M)=A\sin(bM+\phi)$ with

$$A=\left|e^{(-a+ib)c_1}-e^{(-a+ib)c_2}\right|>0.$$

Consequently, if $\widehat M_k=(k\pi-\phi)/b$, then for every sufficiently large $k$ there is an exact singular translation

$$M_k=\widehat M_k+O(e^{-a\widehat M_k}),\qquad D(M_k)=0.$$

This follows directly from the simple zeros of $L$ and the implicit-function theorem (or a sign change on an $O(e^{-a\widehat M_k})$ neighborhood). Hence a translated complex-spectrum design loses rank infinitely often; no global claim $\sigma_3(A_M)=\Theta(e^{-aM})$ is possible. On any phase-separated set $|L(M)|\ge\eta>0$, however,

$$\sigma_1(A_M),\sigma_2(A_M)=\Theta(1),\qquad \sigma_3(A_M)=\Theta(e^{-aM}),\qquad \kappa_2(A_M)=\Theta(e^{aM}).$$

Locally at a sufficiently large $M_k$, the simple zero sharpens this to $\sigma_3(A_M)=\Theta(e^{-aM_k}|M-M_k|)$ and $\kappa_2(A_M)=\Theta(e^{aM_k}/|M-M_k|)$. Thus the ordinary exponential deterioration is the regular-phase behavior, interrupted by reciprocal conditioning walls and exact aliases. This is the translated-maturity form of the eigenvalue-cycle aliasing mechanism studied for irregularly sampled linear systems by Park and Sahai.

For the directed-cycle certificate with offsets $(0.4,1.7)$, $A=0.520001659599$. The first four asymptotic phase zeros are $(3.384584565,7.012183293,10.639782022,14.267380750)$, while the exact singular translations are $(3.385120036,7.012180971,10.639782032,14.267380750)$. The normalized root shift approaches the predicted magnitude $|C|/(bA)=0.085890490311$, and the direct determinant factorization error over 41 translations is below $8.2\times10^{-15}$.

An exact defective example makes the confluent case explicit. Let

$$Q_J=\begin{pmatrix} -8/15&1/15&7/15\\1/3&-2/3&1/3\\1/5&3/5&-4/5 \end{pmatrix},\quad v=(-4,0,4)^\top,\quad w=(-4,1,3)^\top.$$

Every off-diagonal rate is positive, while $Q_Jv=-v$ and $Q_Jw=0.1v-w$, so the eigenvalue $-1$ has a size-two Jordan block. With $F=w$,

$$R(t)=g_0(t)w+0.1g_1(t)v,\qquad g_0(t)=1-e^{-t},\quad g_1(t)=1-(1+t)e^{-t}.$$

The ratio $g_1/g_0$ is strictly increasing because its derivative is $e^{-t}(t-1+e^{-t})/(1-e^{-t})^2>0$. Thus every positive ordered pair works directly, despite the defective generator.

A complex spectrum marks a real boundary to that statement, not merely a limitation of the proof. For the directed three-cycle

$$Q_\circlearrowright=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\0&-1&1\\1&0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad F=(1,-1,0)^\top,$$

the Krylov determinant is $-3$, so almost every pair of maturities identifies the prior. But the nonzero eigenvalues are $-3/2\pm i\sqrt3/2$. If $b=\sqrt3/2$, then at $\tau_1=\pi/b$ and $\tau_2=2\pi/b$ the factors $1-e^{(-3/2+ib)\tau_j}$ are both real. The two real response vectors are therefore collinear and

$$\det[ {\bf1}\ R(2\pi/\sqrt3)\ R(4\pi/\sqrt3)]=0.$$

Thus a cyclic feature still gives generic identifiability, but distinct maturities need not all work once oscillatory modes are present. The theorem assumes a real nonzero spectrum; it does not claim that this condition is necessary for every particular chain.

For three states, however, the boundary is exact. A cyclic centered scalar feature has the all-pairs property

$$\det[ {\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ R(\tau_2)]\ne0 \quad\text{for every }0<\tau_1<\tau_2$$

if and only if the two nonzero eigenvalues are real. Sufficiency is the real-spectrum theorem above. Conversely, write a complex pair as $-a\pm ib$, with $a,b>0$, and identify the centered real plane with $\mathbb C$. Apart from a fixed nonzero feature factor, the response is

$$z(t)={e^{(-a+ib)t}-1\over -a+ib}.$$

At $\tau_1=\pi/b$ and $\tau_2=2\pi/b$, direct substitution gives the exact relation

$$R(2\pi/b)=\left(1-e^{-a\pi/b}\right)R(\pi/b).$$

Hence every three-state complex-spectrum generator has an exceptional positive maturity pair, not just the directed cycle. This converse is special to a two-dimensional centered space; in higher dimensions a complex pair does not by itself force two full response columns to be collinear.

The complete three-state aliasing equation

The obstruction is not a single accidental pair. In the same complex coordinate, the real determinant is a nonzero model-dependent constant times the oriented area $\operatorname{Im}\{\overline{z(\tau_1)}z(\tau_2)\}$. Consequently, for every cyclic centered feature, its complete exceptional set is the zero set of

$$\mathcal K_{a,b}(\tau_1,\tau_2) =e^{-a(\tau_1+\tau_2)}\sin\{b(\tau_2-\tau_1)\} -e^{-a\tau_2}\sin(b\tau_2) +e^{-a\tau_1}\sin(b\tau_1).$$

This formula gives a universal infinite aliasing lattice. Put $\tau_k=k\pi/b$. Since

$$\mathcal K_{a,b}(\tau_k,t) =e^{-at}\left\{(-1)^ke^{-a\tau_k}-1\right\}\sin(bt),$$

every pair $(\tau_k,\tau_\ell)$ with $1\le k<\ell$ is exceptional. Each lattice zero is simple in its second maturity, because

$$\partial_{\tau_2}\mathcal K_{a,b}(\tau_k,\tau_\ell) =b(-1)^\ell e^{-a\tau_\ell} \left\{(-1)^ke^{-a\tau_k}-1\right\}\ne0.$$

Thus each lattice point lies on a locally smooth exceptional curve. Conversely, if $\sin(b\tau_1)\ne0$, every exceptional partner must satisfy the explicit bound

$$\tau_2\le \tau_1+{1\over a} \log\!\left({1+e^{-a\tau_1}\over|\sin(b\tau_1)|}\right).$$

Indeed, beyond this bound the two terms multiplied by $e^{-a\tau_2}$ cannot cancel the nonzero limit $e^{-a\tau_1}\sin(b\tau_1)$. Analyticity then implies that a fixed off-lattice first maturity has only finitely many exceptional partners. The lattice is therefore infinite but nongeneric. This is the same eigenvalue-cycle aliasing mechanism that Park and Sahai show can be broken by nonuniform sampling in linear observation problems.

For the directed-cycle certificate, $\det[{\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ R(\tau_2)] =-(2/\sqrt3)\mathcal K_{3/2,\sqrt3/2}(\tau_1,\tau_2)$. Two hundred arbitrary maturity pairs reproduce this identity within $1.1\times10^{-14}$. Across 100 random positive-rate complex-spectrum generators, all six lattice pairs with $1\le k<\ell\le4$ are singular: the maximum relative determinant is $3.7\times10^{-16}$ and the maximum exact collinearity residual is $4.4\times10^{-16}$.

Generic maturity tuples and exceptional-set fragility

The word generic can be made measure-theoretic, for an arbitrary vector feature and any fixed number $q$ of maturity blocks. On the connected chamber

$$\mathcal C_q=\{(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q):0<\tau_1<\cdots<\tau_q\},$$

let

$$\mathcal A(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_q) =[\,{\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ \cdots\ R(\tau_q)\,], \qquad \rho=\max_{\mathcal C_q}\operatorname{rank}\mathcal A.$$

Generic-rank theorem. The exceptional set

$$\mathcal E_q=\{\tau\in\mathcal C_q: \operatorname{rank}\mathcal A(\tau)<\rho\}$$

has empty interior and Lebesgue measure zero.

Indeed, every entry of $R(\tau)=H(I-e^{Q_0\tau})F$ is real analytic. Choose a maturity tuple attaining rank $\rho$ and one nonzero $\rho\times\rho$ minor there. That minor is a nontrivial real-analytic function on $\mathcal C_q$, and every rank-deficient tuple lies in its zero set. A nontrivial real-analytic function has a null zero set, by Mityagin's short primary proof; it also cannot vanish on an open subset. This argument does not assert that the exceptional set is empty, nor does it give a uniform conditioning bound.

For a cyclic scalar feature, the small-maturity expansion below supplies one full-rank tuple with $q=r=n-1$. Consequently, for an arbitrary real or complex spectrum, almost every ordered positive $r$-tuple identifies the initial prior. The real-spectrum theorem is stronger: it says the exceptional set is empty.

The directed-cycle zero above is locally a smooth codimension-one obstruction, and its numerical instability is sharp. Write

$$\Delta(\tau_1,\tau_2) =\det[\,{\bf1}\ R(\tau_1)\ R(\tau_2)\,], \qquad (\tau_1^\star,\tau_2^\star) =(2\pi/\sqrt3,4\pi/\sqrt3).$$

Since $R'(t)=e^{Q_0t}F$ for the centered feature, direct evaluation gives

$$\partial_{\tau_2}\Delta(\tau_1^\star,\tau_2^\star) =1.885990859775\times10^{-5}\ne0.$$

The implicit-function theorem therefore makes the nearby exceptional set a smooth curve. If $A_0=\mathcal A(\tau_1^\star,\tau_2^\star)$ has nonzero singular values $s_1\ge s_2>0$, then a transverse displacement $\delta$ in the second maturity satisfies

$$\sigma_{\min}\!\left(\mathcal A(\tau_1^\star, \tau_2^\star+\delta)\right) ={|\partial_{\tau_2}\Delta|\over s_1s_2}|\delta|+O(\delta^2),$$ $$\kappa_2\!\left(\mathcal A(\tau_1^\star, \tau_2^\star+\delta)\right) ={s_1^2s_2\over|\partial_{\tau_2}\Delta|} |\delta|^{-1}+O(1).$$

The certified coefficients are $9.409632470013\times10^{-6}$ and $184072.099850$, respectively. At $|\delta|=0.00125$, the measured determinant slope, smallest-singular-value slope, and condition coefficient are all within $0.2\%$ of these limits; the last determinant-slope convergence order is $0.9988$. Thus measure-zero exceptions can still create an exact reciprocal conditioning wall nearby.

The scalar cyclic case also makes the near-zero conditioning explicit. Let $r=n-1$, let $K=[{\bf1}\ F\ Q_0F\ \cdots\ Q_0^{r-1}F]$, and take $\tau_j=\epsilon c_j$ with $0<c_1<\cdots<c_r$. Then

$$\det[ {\bf1}\ R(\epsilon c_1)\ \cdots\ R(\epsilon c_r)] =\det K\, \frac{\left(\prod_jc_j\right) \left(\prod_{i<j}(c_j-c_i)\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^r k!} \epsilon^{r(r+1)/2}\{1+O(\epsilon)\}.$$

Thus distinct sufficiently small maturities do identify a cyclic prior, but their determinant collapses at a precisely polynomial rate. This small-$\tau$ loss is different from the exponential loss at fixed positive maturities as $m\to\infty$.

The determinant gives the product of the singular values but not the actual noise amplification. That hierarchy is also exact. Put $C_\epsilon=[R(\epsilon c_1)\ \cdots\ R(\epsilon c_r)]$ and $A_\epsilon=[{\bf1}\ C_\epsilon]$, and order singular values from largest to smallest. Then, as $\epsilon\downarrow0$,

$$\sigma_k(C_\epsilon)\asymp\epsilon^k\quad(1\le k\le r),\qquad \sigma_{k+1}(A_\epsilon)\asymp\epsilon^k\quad(0\le k\le r),$$ $$\kappa(C_\epsilon)\asymp\epsilon^{-(r-1)},\qquad \kappa(A_\epsilon)\asymp\epsilon^{-r}.$$

Here $x_\epsilon\asymp y_\epsilon$ means their ratio stays between positive constants depending on $Q_0,F$ and the fixed distinct $c_j$, but not on $\epsilon$. The smallest singular value is therefore order $\epsilon^r$. Consequently any fixed coordinate chart on the zero-sum prior contrasts has worst-case observation-noise amplification of order $\epsilon^{-r}$, even though its relative condition number is only order $\epsilon^{-(r-1)}$ after the normalization direction has been removed.

The proof exposes the confluent Vandermonde structure. Cayley–Hamilton gives an exact analytic factorization

$$A_\epsilon =K\,\operatorname{diag}(1,\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon^r) \{V(c)+O(\epsilon)\},$$

where the lower-right block of $V(c)$ is $[c_j^k/k!]_{k,j=1}^r$. It is invertible because the $c_j$ are positive and distinct. Both outer factors remain uniformly invertible, so the standard singular-value inequalities transfer the powers on the diagonal to $A_\epsilon$; deleting the normalization column and using the centered Krylov block gives the statement for $C_\epsilon$. This is the small-node instability of Vandermonde inversion studied classically by Gautschi (1962), here pulled back through the Krylov basis.

The hidden constants in $\asymp$ are also explicit. This matters when maturities are chosen rather than merely proved to exist. Take a QR factorization $K=U_KR_K$ and an LQ factorization $V(c)=L_VU_V$, with positive or negative diagonal entries allowed in the triangular factors. Then the separated powers in the diagonal imply

$$\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}{\sigma_{k+1}(A_\epsilon)\over\epsilon^k} =\left|(R_K)_{k+1,k+1}(L_V)_{k+1,k+1}\right|, \qquad 0\le k\le r.$$

The formula is independent of the sign conventions in QR and LQ. For $C_\epsilon$, replace $K$ by the centered Krylov block $K_c=[F\ Q_0F\ \cdots\ Q_0^{r-1}F]$ and $V(c)$ by $V_c=[c_j^k/k!]_{k,j=1}^r$. If $K_c=U_cR_c$ and $V_c=L_cW_c$, then

$$\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}{\sigma_k(C_\epsilon)\over\epsilon^k} =\left|(R_c)_{kk}(L_c)_{kk}\right|, \qquad 1\le k\le r.$$

To prove the limit, orthogonal invariance reduces the matrix to $R_KD_\epsilon\{L_V+E_\epsilon\}$, where $D_\epsilon=\operatorname{diag}(1,\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon^r)$ and $\|E_\epsilon\|=O(\epsilon)$. In its $j$th exterior power, the unique minor with the smallest exponent uses the first $j$ rows and columns. Therefore the product of the largest $j$ singular values, divided by $\epsilon^{j(j-1)/2}$, converges to the product of the first $j$ absolute diagonal pairs. Dividing consecutive product limits gives the displayed individual constant. The analytic remainder has one additional power in every flag row, so it cannot alter the limit.

Determinant-optimal maturities under a cap

The determinant formula also solves an exact local design problem. Fix a maximum scaled maturity and write $\tau_j=\epsilon T_{\max}c_j$, with $0<c_1<\cdots<c_r\le1$. All model-dependent factors are fixed, so maximizing the magnitude of the leading determinant is equivalent to maximizing

$$\Delta(0,c_1,\ldots,c_r) =\left(\prod_{j=1}^r c_j\right) \left(\prod_{1\le i<j\le r}(c_j-c_i)\right).$$

The unique maximizer is the affine Legendre–Gauss–Lobatto design. Let $-1=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_r=1$, where the interior $x_j$ are the zeros of $P_r'$, the derivative of the degree-$r$ Legendre polynomial. Then

$$c_j^\star={1+x_j\over2},\qquad 1\le j\le r.$$

Indeed, the last node must equal one, and the log Vandermonde is strictly concave in the remaining ordered interior nodes. Its equilibrium equations are $\sum_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)^{-1}=0$. If $w(x)=\prod_{j=0}^r(x-x_j)=(x^2-1)p(x)$, these equations say that $w''$ vanishes at every interior node. Degree and leading-coefficient comparison give $w''=r(r+1)p$, whose polynomial solution is $p\propto P_r'$. Strict concavity proves global uniqueness. This is the interval Fekete geometry originating in Fejér's interpolation work.

This is a determinant or local D-optimality statement. It does not claim that the same nodes maximize the smallest singular value or minimize the condition number. For four states ($r=3$), the normalized design is

$$\left({1-1/\sqrt5\over2}, {1+1/\sqrt5\over2},1\right) =(0.276393202,0.723606798,1).$$

At the cap used in the certificate, it improves the leading determinant by $1.250725436$ over the previous $(0.7,1.3,2.2)/2.2$ design, hence improves the corresponding information determinant by the square, $1.564314115$. At $\epsilon=0.0015625$, the exact response determinant ratio is $1.250158127$. The equilibrium equations are verified through nine states with maximum residual $2.4\times10^{-13}$; the exact gains over equally spaced nodes from three through nine states are $(1,1.086729,1.302945,1.733914,2.568915,4.244826,7.831480)$.

Three states: D-, E-, and condition-optimal designs differ

The caveat is substantive even in the smallest nontrivial case. For three states, write the two normalized maturities as $(c,1)$, $0<c<1$. The response coefficient matrix is

$$V_c=\begin{pmatrix}c&1\\ c^2/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its LQ diagonal entries, in absolute value, are

$$\ell_1(c)=\sqrt{1+c^2},\qquad \ell_2(c)={c(1-c)\over2\sqrt{1+c^2}}.$$

The model-dependent Krylov QR factors multiply these quantities but do not change their optimizers. Consequently three natural local criteria give three different designs:

$$\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{criterion}&\text{objective}&c^\star\\ \hline \text{determinant (D)}&|\det V_c|&1/2\\ \text{weakest direction (E)}&\ell_2(c)& c_E^3+2c_E-1=0\\ \text{response condition}&\ell_1(c)/\ell_2(c)&\sqrt2-1 \end{array}$$

Here $c_E=0.4533976515$. The cubic has exactly one root in $(0,1)$ because its derivative is positive. Direct differentiation gives all three optima and shows they are unique. The condition row concerns the response-only matrix $C_\epsilon$; for the augmented matrix $A_\epsilon$, the largest normalization singular value is node-independent to leading order, so its condition optimum coincides with the E-optimum.

The E-design raises the weakest-direction constant by $0.941474\%$ relative to the D-design. In a cyclic three-state certificate at $\epsilon=0.001$, the measured weakest constants for the D/E/response-condition designs are respectively $(0.0538838754,0.0543941976,0.0540253769)$, while the corresponding response condition coefficients are $(22.5707789,21.9579293,21.7937489)$. Every measured coefficient is within $0.3\%$ of its QR/LQ limit. Thus maximizing the determinant cannot be silently reinterpreted as optimizing the hardest prior direction.

The $T=O(m^{-1})$ boundary layer

If $T_j=\tau_j/m$, then $\mathcal O_m$ is exactly independent of $m$ after the trivial $1/m$ response scaling:

$$m\mathbb E_\nu Z_{\tau_j/m} =\nu H(I-e^{Q_0\tau_j})F.$$

Different $\tau_j$ can rotate a single feature through enough directions to identify the full prior. By contrast, for fixed positive $T_j$ all blocks converge to $HF$. If $\|e^{Q_0t}R\|\le Ce^{-\delta t}$, then $\mathcal O_m$ lies within $O(e^{-m\delta T_{\min}})$ of $[HF\ \cdots\ HF]$. Hence every singular value beyond $\operatorname{rank}F$ is exponentially small: exact finite-$m$ rank can survive, but the extra identification is exponentially ill-conditioned. The sharp theorem above resolves every exponent when the scaled maturities have fixed gaps and a simple real spectrum. This is why the fixed-maturity first-order theorem correctly retains only $\nu HF$.

The actual bond curve has a compatible, but one order smaller, initial layer because $g(0)=0$. Put $T=\tau/m$, $u=\theta-\bar\theta{\bf1}$, and $v=s-\bar s{\bf1}$. A rescaled Duhamel expansion through the first volatility term gives

$$\begin{aligned} \log P_\nu(\tau/m)-\log P_\pi(\tau/m) &=-\frac{\kappa}{m^2}(\nu-\pi)K_1(\tau) +\frac1{2m^3}(\nu-\pi)K_2(\tau)+O_L(m^{-4}),\\ K_1(\tau)&=\int_0^\tau(\tau-s)e^{Q_0s}u\,ds,\\ K_2(\tau)&=\int_0^\tau(\tau-s)^2e^{Q_0s}(\kappa^2u+v)\,ds, \qquad 0\le\tau\le L. \end{aligned}$$

For several scaled maturities, $[K_1(\tau_1)\ \cdots\ K_1(\tau_q)]$ gives the order-$m^{-2}$ rank test. If both displayed coefficients are resolved, append the $K_2$ columns. The next coefficient contains volatility only through the combination $\kappa^2u+v$; it does not separately identify both unknown features without additional information. In the important special case of constant $\theta$, $u=0$ and the order-$m^{-2}$ initial-prior signal vanishes. Switched variance then becomes the leading signal at order $m^{-3}$, and the augmented rank of $[K_2(\tau_1)\ \cdots\ K_2(\tau_q)]$ determines initial-prior identification.

In the certificate, one centered feature on a nonreversible three-state chain has fixed rank one, yet two scaled maturities $\tau=(0.4,1.7)$ give observability rank two and recover the prior $(0.15,0.55,0.30)$. The exact mean formula agrees with independent quadrature within $1.4\times10^{-16}$. After subtracting the $m^{-2}$ term, the bond-price remainder has orders $2.9997$ and $2.9983$; after also subtracting the new $m^{-3}$ term, these improve to $4.0015$ and $4.0009$. A separate positive-variance, constant-$\theta$ example recovers the same prior from volatility alone, with remainder orders $3.9988$ and $3.9946$ after its leading $m^{-3}$ term. At fixed maturities, the second singular value falls from $4.47\times10^{-2}$ at $m=2$ to $1.05\times10^{-6}$ at $m=16$, displaying the predicted loss of conditioning.

A separate four-state certificate has one scalar cyclic feature. Three maturities $(0.25,0.8,1.9)$ recover the prior $(0.10,0.25,0.40,0.25)$ to $2\times10^{-13}$, while 32 random finite-chain cases verify equality of the sampled response and Krylov ranks. The normalized small-maturity determinant approaches its Vandermonde coefficient and the QR/LQ formula predicts all seven augmented and response-only leading singular-value constants; at $\epsilon=0.00625$ the largest relative discrepancies are $1.44\%$ and $1.44\%$ respectively. The augmented constants are $(2,4.65744565,0.80736962,0.01315675)$ and the response-only constants are $(4.68629884,0.80766818,0.01331125)$. The normalized determinant converges with measured order $0.9725$. The four augmented singular values have measured exponents $(0.000005,0.9823,1.9790,2.9838)$, while the response-only condition number has exponent $2.0015$, verifying the full hierarchy rather than only its product. For a four-state complete-graph generator, whose nonzero eigenvalue has multiplicity three, the scalar-feature response rank remains exactly one at every maturity, confirming the spectral obstruction.

The all-maturities certificate adds a circulation to a four-state reversible chain. Detailed balance fails by $0.05$, while all three nonzero eigenvalues remain real and simple. Across 200 randomly ordered positive maturity triples, it verifies the determinant factorization and its fixed sign; the maximum relative discrepancy is below $5\times10^{-13}$. A representative prior is recovered within $2.2\times10^{-15}$. A separate irreducible three-state chain has the genuine Jordan relations $Q_0v=-v$ and $Q_0w=0.1v-w$. With $F=w$, its Krylov determinant is $1.2$; 200 ordered maturity pairs verify the confluent factorization within $5\times10^{-13}$, and recover a prior within $4.2\times10^{-15}$. For the directed-cycle counterexample, the Krylov determinant is $-3$, a generic pair has response determinant $-0.170100185$, but the exceptional pair has determinant $4.46\times10^{-16}$ and smallest singular value $1.75\times10^{-16}$. Finally, 100 random irreducible three-state chains with complex spectra verify the universal exceptional-pair ratio: the maximum collinearity residual is $5.8\times10^{-16}$, while the smallest relative determinant at a generic comparison pair remains above $0.029$.

A computable finite-rate bound

The following deliberately conservative estimate makes the $O(m^{-2})$ statement explicit, including $T=0$. Use the row-sum operator norm $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. Since $Q_0$ is finite and irreducible, choose $C,\delta>0$ such that

$$\|e^{Q_0t}R\|_\infty\le C e^{-\delta t},\qquad t\ge0.$$

These constants can be certified numerically: select $t_0>0$ with $\rho=\|e^{Q_0t_0}R\|_\infty<1$, then take $C=2/\rho$ and $\delta=-\log\rho/t_0$. On $[0,L]$ set $f(t)=g(t)-(\pi g(t)){\bf1}$, $G=\sup\|f(t)\|_\infty$ and $J=\sup\|f'(t)\|_\infty$. For the model here, for example, one may take $G=\|u\|_\infty+\|v\|_\infty/(2\kappa^2)$ and $J=\kappa\|u\|_\infty+\|v\|_\infty/\kappa$. Write

$$\alpha=\frac{C}{m\delta},\qquad W=\alpha^2e^{GL}(4G^2+J+2\alpha G^3).$$

If $4\alpha G e^{2GL}\le1$ (automatically true when $G=0$), then for every $0\le T\le L$ the remainders in the theorem obey

$$|E_\pi(T)|\le LG e^{GL}W,\qquad |E_i(T)|\le (LG+1)e^{GL}W+4\alpha^2G^2e^{4GL}.$$

These are analytic upper bounds, not confidence intervals for noisy bond quotes. Their rate threshold and constants are intentionally loose; they establish uniform finite-rate control for an arbitrary irreducible finite chain, including a nonreversible one. The certificate checks both bounds and the $m^{-2}$ numerical behavior on the three-state example.

Proof

Expansion and error. Divide $a(t)$ by $\exp(\int_0^t\bar g)$ and call the resulting vector $b$. Decompose it as $b=p{\bf1}+z$, with $p=\pi b$ and $\pi z=0$. Then

$$p'=\pi[fz],\qquad z'=mQ_0z+p f+R\,\operatorname{diag}(f)z,\qquad p(0)=1,\ z(0)=0.$$

Positivity of the finite-state Feynman–Kac functional gives $e^{-GL}\le p\le e^{GL}$. Write $h=Hf$, so $Q_0h=-f$ and $\|h\|\le CG/\delta$, $\|h'\|\le CJ/\delta$. The semigroup estimate and variation of constants, with $4\alpha G\le1$, give $\sup\|z\|\le 2\alpha G e^{GL}$. For $w=z-ph/m$, the condition $f(0)=0$ gives $w(0)=0$, and

$$w'=mQ_0w+R\,\operatorname{diag}(f)z -\frac1m(h'p+hp').$$

Here $\|R\|_\infty\le2$ and $|p'|\le G\|z\|$. Another variation-of-constants estimate gives $\sup\|w\|\le W$. Consequently

$$\left|(\log p)'-\frac1m\pi[fh]\right| \le G e^{GL}W.$$

Integration proves the first bound. Since $b_i=p(1+z_i/p)$, $|z_i/p|\le2\alpha Ge^{2GL}\le1/2$, and $|\log(1+q)-q|\le q^2$ for $|q|\le1/2$, the second bound follows. Substituting $f=-\kappa uB+vB^2/2$ into $\pi[fh]$ yields the three Green–Kubo coefficients and the displayed known-start term.

Information and rank. If $\sum_{j=1}^4 d_j I_j(T)=0$ on an open interval, differentiation gives $\sum_{j=1}^4d_j B(T)^j=0$. Because $B$ ranges over an interval, every $d_j$ vanishes. The same argument with the constant polynomial term proves independence of $T,I_1,\ldots,I_4$. Finally, the long-run covariance rate of additive functionals is $2\mathcal K_0^{\rm sym}$. For an irreducible finite chain this quadratic form is strictly positive for any nonzero centered vector: writing $h=Hf$, the martingale quadratic variation is $\sum_{i,j}\pi_i(Q_0)_{ij}(h_j-h_i)^2$, which vanishes only if $h$ is constant along every transition, hence $f=0$. Thus the Gram matrix of centered regime features has precisely their linear rank, at most $n-1$. For two features this proves the latent determinant restriction, including the two-state equality case.

Initial mixture and boundary layer. Since $Q_0H=H Q_0=\Pi-I$ and $\pi F=0$, integration of $\nu e^{mQ_0t}F$ gives the exact transient-mean formula. Stacking its values gives $\mathcal O_m$, and the augmented rank criterion is simply injectivity on the affine hyperplane $\nu{\bf1}=1$. Exponential mixing proves the fixed-maturity singular-value bound by comparison with the repeated block $HF$. Moreover, on centered columns, $HQ_0=-I$, so the analytic expansion is $R(\tau)=\tau F+\tau^2Q_0F/2!+\cdots$. Its derivatives at zero generate the Krylov space, while Cayley–Hamilton stops that space at power $n-2$; this proves both inclusions in the whole-curve theorem. In the scalar cyclic case, expressing the response columns in the Krylov basis and taking the lowest-degree determinant gives the displayed Vandermonde coefficient. For the yield boundary, set $t=r/m$ in the backward equation and expand

$$g(r/m)=-\frac{\kappa r\theta}{m} +\frac{r^2(\kappa^2\theta+s)}{2m^2}+O_L(m^{-3}).$$

The rescaled equation therefore has potential terms first at orders $m^{-2}$ and $m^{-3}$. Variation of constants around $e^{Q_0r}$ gives the two displayed integrals after changing variables and subtracting the stationary start. A product of two potential insertions, and hence the first quadratic Dyson term, cannot occur before order $m^{-4}$; the quadratic term from the logarithm also first occurs there. This proves the $O_L(m^{-4})$ remainder uniformly for $\tau$ in a fixed compact interval.

This finite-state proof is the elementary Poisson-equation version of the additive-functional central-limit framework. For broader state spaces see Kipnis and Varadhan (1986) in the reversible case and Glynn and Meyn (1996) for irreducible discrete- and continuous-time Markov processes.

Scope: common fixed $\kappa$, fixed known $x$, finite $n$, and a fixed bounded maturity interval. The exact fixed-feature covariance-rank theorem permits any initial prior. The pricing-identification formulas cover stationarity, a labeled starting state, and an unobserved fixed initial mixture. The Krylov theorem characterizes noiseless mean observability across the complete scaled-maturity curve; its determinant formula and the real-spectrum all-maturity sharpening also warn that formal rank need not imply stable statistical recovery. The boundary theorem concerns $T=\tau/m$ with $\tau$ in a fixed compact interval; it is distinct from the fixed-maturity outer theorem. Stochastic reversion speed and simultaneous limits $T\to\infty$ require separate analyses. The integrated-loading theorem only requires $G\succeq0$ and square-integrable deterministic $L$; its finite-basis equality assumes the scalar loading Gram matrix $\Gamma$ is positive definite.
