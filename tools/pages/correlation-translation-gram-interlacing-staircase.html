Rare-tail Gram modes interlace at every staircase level

Between consecutive collision powers, Gaussian modes separate before the tail contrast.

The integer boundaries form rank-two collision blocks. Every open interval between them has a complementary ordering: finitely many Gaussian modes, then one tail mode, with explicit coefficients.

Interlacing-staircase theorem

Fix an integer $m\ge0$, $c>0$, distinct real nodes $x_1,\ldots,x_k$, $1\le r\le k-m-3$, a slope $\tau>0$, and weights $q_1,q_2>0$. Put $Q=q_1+q_2$, $\gamma_1=-q_2/Q$, and $\gamma_2=q_1/Q$.

Let $\varepsilon_n,\delta_n\downarrow0$ satisfy

$$\frac{\delta_n}{\varepsilon_n^m}\longrightarrow0, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_n^{m+1}}{\delta_n}\longrightarrow0.$$

Thus $\varepsilon_n^{m+1}\ll\delta_n\ll\varepsilon_n^m$. Use the Gaussian-plus-two-atom law

$$\tau_{\ell n}=\tau+\gamma_\ell\delta_n,\qquad A_{\ell n}=\exp(\tau_{\ell n}/\varepsilon_n),\qquad p_{\ell n}=q_\ell\varepsilon_n^{2r}A_{\ell n}^{-2c},$$

and let $K_n$ be the covariance Gram of $|X_n|^{c+\varepsilon_nx_j}$.

Write $\phi_a=(x_j^a/a!)_j$, $v=(e^{\tau x_j})_j$, and $\dot v=(x_je^{\tau x_j})_j$. For $s\ge0$, let $P_s$ project orthogonally away from

$$L_s=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_0,\ldots,\phi_{r+s},v\}.$$

For $G\sim N(0,1)$, center $Z_j=|G|^c(\log|G|)^j$ and let $h_j$ be the residual variance of $Z_j$ after projection onto $Z_0,\ldots,Z_{j-1}$.

Exactly $m$ post-tail Gaussian modes occur above the tail scale:

$$\lambda_{r+2+a}(K_n) =h_{r+a}\|P_{a-1}\phi_{r+a}\|^2\varepsilon_n^{2(r+a)} +o(\varepsilon_n^{2(r+a)}),\qquad 1\le a\le m.$$

The statement is empty when $m=0$. After these modes, put $b_m=P_m\dot v$. Then

$$\lambda_{r+m+3}(K_n)=\frac{q_1q_2}{Q}\|b_m\|^2 \varepsilon_n^{2r}\delta_n^2 +o(\varepsilon_n^{2r}\delta_n^2).$$

The first $r$ Gaussian coefficients and two primary order-$\varepsilon_n^{2r}$ coefficients are unchanged. Every remaining eigenvalue is $o(\varepsilon_n^{2r}\delta_n^2)$.

Why the ordering is complete

Successive Schur complements remove $L_0,\ldots,L_m$. At step $a\le m$, Gaussian Gram–Schmidt leaves a feature of size $\varepsilon_n^{r+a}$, while the tail contrast has size $\varepsilon_n^r\delta_n$.

The tail/Gaussian ratio is $\delta_n/\varepsilon_n^a$. It vanishes for every $a\le m$ because $\delta_n/\varepsilon_n^m\to0$, proving the separated Gaussian limits.

After $L_m$ is removed, weighted tail-label centering leaves $\varepsilon_n^r\delta_n\sqrt{q_1q_2/Q}\,b_m$. The next Gaussian feature is smaller by $\varepsilon_n^{m+1}/\delta_n\to0$, and higher tail-confluence features are smaller by $\delta_n\to0$.

Finite-dimensional spectral perturbation now gives the tail limit and the remainder. The coefficient is positive because the evaluations of $1,x,\ldots,x^{r+m},e^{\tau x},xe^{\tau x}$ are independent at $k\ge r+m+3$ distinct nodes by the extended Chebyshev property.

First new interval

Take $m=2$, $r=1$, $c=\tau=q_1=1$, $q_2=2$, $x=(-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3)$, $\varepsilon_n=1/n$, and $\delta_n=\varepsilon_n^{5/2}$. The certificate resolves fourth- and sixth-order Gaussian modes, the seventh-order tail mode, and the smaller eighth-order remainder.

The Gaussian coefficients are $(0.158456045596511,0.030054217288597)$ and the tail coefficient is $0.042089419114059$. At $n=4096$, their exact normalized ratios are $(1.000054109718,1.006570038903,0.993565034524)$.

The next eigenvalue divided by the tail scale is $0.000000020372$. Every number comes from exact Gaussian moments and exact two-atom mixture algebra evaluated at 240 digits.

Scope and literature

This is a sequencewise theorem for the displayed triangular laws at one fixed positive maturity. It treats each fixed $m$ and the two stated limiting ratios; it gives neither a finite-$n$ bound nor uniformity in $m$ or over a moving separation window.

The result concerns the nonlinear output covariance Gram. It changes neither fixed-feature Green–Kubo rank nor rank obtained after integrating different maturity loadings.

Gautschi supplies the column-confluence geometry, Kato supplies finite-dimensional asymptotic perturbation theory, and Karlin and Studden supply the zero-count argument.
